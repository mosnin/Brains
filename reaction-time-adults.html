Reaction Time for Adults: Norms from 18 to 65

Adult reaction time peaks around age 22–25 and gradually slows. Learn the norms for every adult age group and what you can do to maintain sharp reflexes.

The Adult Reaction Time Trajectory

Reaction time follows a characteristic lifespan trajectory: rapid improvement through childhood and adolescence, a peak plateau in the early-to-mid twenties, and a gradual, continuous slowing thereafter. By the time a person reaches 65, simple reaction time is typically 25–30% slower than at the peak in the mid-twenties — a change that accumulates imperceptibly year by year rather than arriving as a sudden decline.

Understanding where you fall within your age group's normal range is far more meaningful than comparing yourself to the population-wide average, which aggregates across a wide age span and can be misleading for any individual age bracket.

Adult Reaction Time Norms by Age Group

The following norms are derived from multiple large-scale studies and represent median simple visual RT and two-choice RT values for healthy, unimpaired adults tested under standard conditions. Individual variation within each age group is substantial — roughly ±50 ms around the median.

Age Group Simple RT Range Choice RT Range Influencing Factors
18–24 230–260 ms 360–410 ms Peak performance, high plasticity
25–34 240–275 ms 375–430 ms Stable, high performance; minimal decline
35–44 255–290 ms 395–455 ms Mild slowing begins; lifestyle factors more influential
45–54 270–310 ms 415–480 ms Measurable decline; cardiovascular health critical
55–64 290–335 ms 445–520 ms Significant slowing; training benefits grow larger

How to Read This Table

These ranges represent the middle 50% of the healthy adult population in each age group. If your score falls in the lower portion of your age range, you are performing at or above average for your age. Scores faster than the lower bound are excellent for your age group.

Gender Differences in Adult Reaction Time

Across large samples, males consistently demonstrate faster simple and choice reaction times than females by approximately 10–15 ms on average. This difference is small but statistically robust across studies and age groups. It persists after controlling for height, muscle strength, and motor response time, suggesting a genuine difference in neural processing speed rather than purely peripheral factors.

The gender gap in RT is largest in simple RT tasks (where speed dominates) and narrower in tasks requiring accuracy alongside speed. It also does not fully explain individual variation — many females outperform many males, and the overlap between distributions is substantial.

The biological basis is not fully established but likely involves a combination of hormonal effects on neural myelination, differences in baseline arousal, and subtle differences in cortical motor preparation. Practically speaking, the gender difference is small enough that a woman scoring 250 ms and a man scoring 235 ms are performing equivalently relative to their respective norms.

How Exercise, Sleep, and Diet Affect Adult Reaction Time

Aerobic Exercise

Aerobic exercise is the single most consistently documented lifestyle factor that preserves and improves reaction time across the adult lifespan. A 2016 meta-analysis of 24 randomised controlled trials found that aerobic training reduced simple RT by an average of approximately 20 ms and choice RT by approximately 30 ms compared to sedentary controls. The mechanisms include increased cerebral blood flow, BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) release, white matter preservation, and improved dopaminergic tone in motor control circuits.

Even a single bout of moderate aerobic exercise (20–30 minutes at 60–70% of maximum heart rate) produces acute RT improvements lasting 1–2 hours post-exercise. Long-term adaptation requires sustained aerobic conditioning — at least three sessions per week over 8–12 weeks to observe reliable improvements on laboratory RT tests.

Sleep Quality

For working adults, sleep quality is often the largest single modifiable determinant of day-to-day RT performance. The optimal sleep duration for RT maintenance is 7–9 hours for most adults. Consistently sleeping 6 hours or fewer per night accumulates a progressive RT deficit that is not fully reversed by a single recovery night. Adults who consistently prioritise sleep demonstrate RT profiles that are often 20–30 ms faster than sleep-restricted counterparts of the same age.

Diet and Hydration

Dehydration of as little as 2% of body weight slows simple RT by approximately 10–20 ms. High-glycaemic meals can cause RT impairment during the subsequent hypoglycaemic dip. Long-term dietary patterns rich in omega-3 fatty acids, polyphenols (found in berries and dark chocolate), and B vitamins are associated with better RT in observational studies, likely through anti-inflammatory and vascular mechanisms.

How Gaming and Sports Affect Adult Reaction Time

Action video games — specifically first-person shooters and real-time strategy games requiring rapid multi-target tracking — provide one of the most consistently documented training effects on adult RT. In landmark studies by Daphne Bavelier's lab, experienced action gamers show simple and choice RT advantages of 30–50 ms over non-gamers of the same age, and these advantages generalise to laboratory tasks that differ from the trained games.

Sports training, particularly in sports with high reaction demands (cricket batting, table tennis, boxing, motor racing), similarly produces both sport-specific and some general RT improvements. The distinguishing feature of effective RT training is that it must involve genuine time pressure: practice that allows unlimited response time does not improve RT.

Practical Tip for Adults

To maintain or improve your RT as you age: prioritise 7–8 hours of sleep, perform aerobic exercise at least 3 times per week, stay well-hydrated, and spend 10–15 minutes per day on a variety of reaction-based tasks. Even short daily sessions on reaction time games show measurable improvement within 2–3 weeks of consistent practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good reaction time for a 30-year-old?

For a healthy 30-year-old, a simple visual reaction time of 240–270 ms is typical and good. A result below 220 ms would be excellent. The mid-twenties mark peak RT performance; by 30, there is usually only a small, barely noticeable slowing of around 10–15 ms compared to peak. Choice RT for a 30-year-old averages around 380–430 ms.

Does coffee improve reaction time?

Yes, caffeine reliably reduces reaction time by approximately 10–30 ms on average. The effect is most pronounced in individuals who are sleep-deprived, caffeine-naive, or regular consumers who have abstained overnight. A typical dose of 100–200 mg (one to two cups of coffee) is sufficient to produce a measurable benefit within 30–60 minutes.

Can adults maintain fast reaction times through training?

Yes. Regular practice with reaction time tasks, video games (particularly action games), and sport-specific training all help maintain faster RT in adults. Research shows that experienced action video game players in their 30s and 40s perform comparably to untrained 20-year-olds on simple and choice RT tasks. Aerobic exercise also preserves RT by maintaining cerebrovascular health.
