Is Your Reaction Time Good?

Depending on your age and test type, "good" reaction time varies significantly. This guide shows you exactly where your RT score falls — and what it actually means for your cognitive health.

Simple Reaction Time Score Ranges

Simple reaction time (SRT) measures the time between a single stimulus appearing — usually a colored target — and your response. It is the purest measure of neural processing speed with no decision-making component. Use the table below to find where your score falls.

Simple Reaction Time — Score Classification (Visual Stimulus)
Score (ms) Category What It Means
<180ms Exceptional Elite level — typical of professional athletes and esports competitors
180–220ms Excellent Top quartile of healthy adults; well-trained nervous system
220–260ms Good Above average for healthy adults; solid reflexes
260–320ms Average Normal for most healthy adults; nothing to be concerned about
>320ms Below Average Slower than typical; often due to fatigue, age, or lack of practice

Choice Reaction Time Score Ranges

Choice reaction time (CRT) requires you to select the correct response among two or more options. It adds a decision-making layer on top of raw speed, so scores are naturally higher than simple RT. The 2-choice version is the most commonly used benchmark.

Choice Reaction Time — Score Classification (2-Choice Task)
Score (ms) Category What It Means
<300ms Exceptional Very fast decision-making; top-tier cognitive speed
300–380ms Excellent Well above average; fast and accurate decision making
380–440ms Good Above average for most adult age groups
440–540ms Average Normal and expected for healthy adults aged 25–55
>540ms Poor Below typical adult range; may indicate fatigue or need for practice
Testing tip: A single test is not diagnostic. Try 5–10 trials and use your median score for best accuracy. Outlier fast or slow trials can distort your average significantly — the median is far more resistant to flukes.

How to Interpret Your Specific Score

Raw milliseconds are only meaningful in context. Here is what to consider when reading your result:

Are you comparing to the right test?

Simple RT and choice RT scores are not interchangeable. A score of 320ms is average for simple RT but excellent for choice RT. Always identify which type of test you took before comparing to benchmarks.

How many trials did you take?

Your first 1–3 trials in any session are typically 30–50ms slower than your settled performance due to warm-up effects. If the test only ran 3 trials, your score likely underestimates your true speed. Longer tests with 10+ trials give a more representative result.

What were your testing conditions?

The device you use matters. Touchscreen responses are generally 20–40ms slower than keyboard/mouse responses. Display refresh rate also affects stimulus timing precision. BrainForge tests are optimized for accuracy, but hardware variation is real.

Age-Adjusted Benchmarks

The ranges above reflect healthy adults aged approximately 18–35. Reaction time naturally slows with age — roughly 1–2ms per year after the mid-20s peak. This means a score that looks "average" for a 25-year-old may be "excellent" for a 55-year-old. For a full breakdown of expected scores by decade, see our reaction time by age guide.

As a quick reference: adults aged 55–65 typically score 300–380ms on simple RT, and a score in the 260–300ms range for that age group is genuinely exceptional. Do not penalize yourself by comparing to 20-year-old norms.

What a "Slow" RT Might Indicate

If your score falls in the below-average range, the most likely explanations are entirely reversible:

  • Sleep deprivation: Even mild sleep restriction (6 hours instead of 8) measurably slows RT. After 24 hours without sleep, RT declines by 30–40%.
  • Dehydration: A 2% reduction in body water is enough to impair cognitive speed. Drink water before testing.
  • Stress and anxiety: Acute stress tightens attention, but chronic stress depletes the attentional resources needed for fast, accurate responses.
  • Caffeine timing: If you normally consume caffeine but skipped it before testing, you may experience a temporary withdrawal-related slowing.
  • Unfamiliar interface: First-time users are slower simply because the task is novel. Familiarity alone can improve scores by 20–40ms.

A slow score on an online test is almost never a sign of neurological disease on its own. It becomes meaningful only when it is consistently slow across many sessions and accompanied by real-world difficulties.

Key Takeaway: Most healthy adults fall in the 230–300ms range for simple reaction time. Anything in that range is completely normal. If you scored in the 260–300ms window, your reflexes are exactly where they should be — focus on consistency across trials rather than chasing a single fast number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered a fast reaction time?

For simple visual RT, below 200ms is considered exceptional and is typically seen only in professional athletes and trained esports players. Below 220ms is excellent, placing you in the top quartile of healthy young adults. In everyday terms, anything under 250ms is fast by any adult standard.

What is a below-average reaction time?

For simple RT, above 320ms is below average for a healthy adult under 50. For 2-choice RT, above 540ms is considered poor. Age matters enormously — a 65-year-old scoring 340ms on simple RT is well within their normal range and should not compare themselves to young-adult benchmarks.

Should I be worried about a slow reaction time?

Probably not. A single slow online test result is almost always caused by temporary factors: fatigue, stress, dehydration, or an unfamiliar test interface. If you score slowly consistently across many sessions over days or weeks, and you notice real-world difficulties like slower driving reactions or delayed responses in conversation, that pattern is worth mentioning to a healthcare provider. Online tests are screening tools, not diagnostics.
