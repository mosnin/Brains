Is Your Memory Score Good?

Memory test scores depend entirely on which test you took. A digit span of 6 and an N-Back level of 2 both reflect "average" working memory — but through completely different lenses. This guide explains what your specific score means across each test type.

Verbal vs. Visuospatial Memory — They Measure Different Things

Digit Span tests the verbal/phonological side of working memory — the mental "inner voice" that rehearses sequences of sounds or words. Corsi Block tests the visuospatial side — the mental "inner eye" that tracks locations in space.

These two systems are functionally independent. It is entirely possible — and common — to score well on one and only average on the other. A musician may have exceptional verbal span from years of memorizing notated sequences. An architect may have exceptional spatial span from working with floor plans. Neither domain automatically predicts the other.

If your scores differ across test types, that is not a red flag — it reflects the natural specialization of your working memory system based on your life experiences and cognitive profile.

Practice Effects — First-Try Scores Are Always Lower

Working memory tests have a significant practice effect: your score on the first attempt is almost always lower than your score after a few sessions. This is not cheating — it reflects genuine learning of the task format, not underlying memory capacity.

For digit span, first-session scores can be 1–2 digits lower than stabilized performance. For N-Back, novice users often score at 1-Back despite having average working memory, simply because the task is cognitively demanding and unfamiliar. Give yourself 3–5 sessions before drawing conclusions about your ability.

This also means your scores can and should improve over time — see our guide on how to improve working memory for evidence-based strategies.

When a Low Memory Score Warrants Concern

A low score on a single session rarely warrants concern. The pattern that matters is:

  • Consistent low scores across multiple sessions on different days
  • Declining scores over weeks or months when retesting
  • Real-world difficulties such as forgetting recent conversations, losing track of tasks, or missing appointments that didn't previously cause problems
  • Recent onset in an otherwise previously strong performer

Any one of these is reason to speak with a healthcare professional — not because an online test diagnosed anything, but because the pattern deserves proper clinical evaluation.

Key Takeaway: A forward digit span of 5–6 is completely normal for most adults. Backward span of 4–5 reflects good executive control. If you scored at or above these marks, your working memory is healthy. If you scored below, take the test again after adequate sleep and without distractions before drawing any conclusions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 7 a good digit span score?

Yes — a forward digit span of 7 is solidly above average. The classic cognitive psychology finding is that most adults hold 7 ± 2 items in verbal working memory. A score of 7 is in the "Good" range, and 8–9 is excellent. The true average across healthy adults is closer to 5–6 digits.

What does backward digit span actually test?

Backward digit span requires you to mentally reverse a sequence before recalling it. This engages the central executive — the "manager" of working memory — rather than just passive storage. It is a more demanding and clinically informative measure than forward span, and is included in many neuropsychological batteries for this reason. Expect your backward score to be 1–2 digits lower than your forward score; that gap is normal and expected.

My memory score seems low — what should I do?

Start with the most likely causes: were you tired, stressed, or distracted during testing? Retake the test on a different day under better conditions. If scores remain consistently low across multiple attempts and you notice real-world memory difficulties, that pattern is worth discussing with your doctor or a neuropsychologist. Online memory tests are screening tools — they flag a pattern worth investigating, not a diagnosis.
