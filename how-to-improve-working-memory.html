Working Memory

How to Improve Working Memory: 7 Evidence-Based Strategies

Working memory is trainable. From N-Back training and aerobic exercise to mindfulness and sleep, here are seven strategies with research backing and practical protocols.

Why Working Memory Matters

Working memory is the cognitive system that holds and manipulates a small amount of information over short periods — roughly the mental workbench you use for reading comprehension, mental arithmetic, following instructions, and making decisions. Baddeley and Hitch's influential model (1974, revised 2000) describes it as comprising a central executive, a phonological loop, a visuospatial sketchpad, and an episodic buffer. It is one of the strongest predictors of academic performance, professional success, and fluid intelligence.

The good news: working memory capacity is not fixed. Research over the past two decades has identified specific, replicable methods to expand it — both in terms of absolute capacity and in terms of how efficiently you use it.

7 Evidence-Based Strategies

1. N-Back Training

The dual N-Back task requires you to monitor a sequence of visual positions and auditory sounds, pressing a key whenever the current item matches the one from N steps back. It taxes the central executive and updating functions heavily. Jaeggi et al. (2008) showed that 19 sessions of dual N-Back training produced significant gains in fluid intelligence (measured by matrices tests), a finding that attracted enormous attention. Subsequent meta-analyses have found moderate, reliable effects on working memory capacity. The key is consistency: gains require sustained practice, not occasional sessions.

2. Chunking

Chunking is the cognitive strategy of grouping individual items into meaningful clusters. George Miller's (1956) famous "magical number seven" paper established that working memory can hold about 7±2 chunks, not individual items. A phone number — 4-1-5-5-5-5-0-1-2 — is nine items if processed as digits, but three chunks if processed as area code, exchange, and subscriber number. Chunking is available immediately and effectively multiplies working memory capacity. Learning domain-specific chunks (medical terminology, chess patterns, music theory) is one reason experts outperform novices on domain-relevant WM tasks.

3. Aerobic Exercise

Regular aerobic exercise produces some of the most robust and generalised improvements to working memory across all age groups, all training durations, and all working memory measures. The mechanisms include elevated BDNF (which promotes neurogenesis in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex), improved cardiovascular delivery of oxygen and glucose to neural tissue, and reduced cortisol. A meta-analysis by Verburgh et al. (2014) confirmed significant positive effects of acute and chronic exercise on executive functions including working memory. Even a 20-minute brisk walk before a working memory task produces immediate measurable improvements.

4. Sleep Optimisation

Sleep is arguably the most underappreciated working memory intervention. During slow-wave sleep, the hippocampus replays and consolidates memories formed during waking hours, transferring them to more stable cortical representations. During REM sleep, emotional and procedural memories are processed and integrated. A single night of sleep deprivation reduces working memory performance by 20–40% on complex tasks. Prioritising 7–9 hours is not optional for anyone serious about cognitive performance; it is the foundation on which all other interventions depend.

5. Mindfulness Meditation

In a landmark study, Zeidan et al. (2010) showed that just four days of mindfulness meditation (20 minutes per day) produced significant improvements in working memory, sustained attention, and cognitive flexibility compared to a control group. The mechanism is thought to involve reduced mind-wandering (which consumes working memory resources) and enhanced attentional control by the prefrontal cortex. Longer-term meditators show structural differences in prefrontal grey matter density. Even brief daily practice accumulates into measurable cognitive gains.

6. Reduce Cognitive Load

Working memory is a limited resource. When you reduce unnecessary demands on it — by eliminating irrelevant distractions, organising your environment, using external memory aids for low-priority information, and avoiding multitasking — you free capacity for the tasks that matter. This is the principle behind cognitive load theory in educational psychology. Practically, this means: turn off notifications when doing demanding cognitive work, use to-do lists to offload low-priority items from working memory, and avoid background conversation or music with lyrics during high-WM tasks.

7. Nutritional Support

Several nutritional factors have evidence-based associations with working memory performance. Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) are essential components of neuronal membranes and support synaptic plasticity, with meta-analyses showing small but consistent cognitive benefits. Vitamin B12 deficiency impairs myelination and is associated with working memory decline, particularly in older adults. Blueberry consumption has accumulated pilot-study evidence for memory benefits, attributed to anthocyanin flavonoids that cross the blood-brain barrier and reduce oxidative stress in hippocampal tissue. Adequate hydration (even mild dehydration reduces cognitive performance) should not be overlooked.

Recommended Training Protocol: 20–25 minutes of N-Back training, 4–5 days per week, for a minimum of 4 weeks is the protocol with the strongest evidence base for working memory improvement. Combine with 20–30 minutes of aerobic exercise on the same or alternate days for synergistic effects.

Training Games for Working Memory

  • Dual N-Back — the gold-standard WM training task
  • Digit Span — measure and train phonological loop
  • Corsi Block — train visuospatial working memory
  • Spatial N-Back — visual updating and sequencing
  • Letter-Number Sequencing — central executive training

Recommended Training Schedule

For best results, combine task-specific WM training with supporting lifestyle interventions:

  • Monday / Wednesday / Friday / Saturday: 20–25 min N-Back or Corsi Block training + 25 min aerobic exercise
  • Tuesday / Thursday: Active recovery — 10 min mindfulness meditation, focus on breath and mental noting
  • Daily: 7–9 hours of sleep, omega-3 supplement, hydration
  • Ongoing: Use chunking deliberately in high-WM demands tasks; use external memory systems for low-priority information
Key Takeaway: The three highest-return working memory interventions are sleep (free and immediate), aerobic exercise (broad and lasting), and targeted N-Back training (specific and cumulative). Stack all three before adding supplements or advanced techniques.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can working memory really be improved?

Yes, though the extent depends on which aspect you are training. Task-specific working memory capacity shows clear and robust improvements with targeted practice. Transfer to other cognitive tasks is more debated but is supported by several meta-analyses, particularly for near-transfer tasks. Aerobic exercise and sleep optimisation show the broadest and most reliable generalised improvements to working memory across all age groups.

How long does working memory training take to show results?

Task-specific improvements appear within 1–2 weeks. Broader gains — improved performance on untrained working memory tasks — typically require 4–6 weeks of consistent training at 20–25 minutes per session, 4–5 days per week. The Jaeggi et al. (2008) dual N-Back study showed significant fluid intelligence gains after 19–20 sessions. Aerobic exercise benefits to working memory appear after 4–8 weeks of regular training.

Does stress reduce working memory?

Yes, acutely and chronically. Acute stress floods the prefrontal cortex with norepinephrine and dopamine at levels that impair rather than enhance working memory function. Chronic stress chronically elevates cortisol, which damages dendritic spines in the prefrontal cortex and reduces grey matter density in regions critical for working memory. Stress management is therefore a legitimate and often overlooked working memory intervention.
