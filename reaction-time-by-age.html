Reaction Time by Age: Complete Norms and Charts

Reaction time — the interval between a stimulus and your response — changes predictably across the human lifespan. Understanding where you fall relative to age-matched norms helps you set realistic goals and track meaningful improvement over time.

~350ms Children (8–12)
~250ms Young Adults (18–24)
~280ms Middle-aged (35–54)
~350ms Seniors (65–74)

How Reaction Time Changes Across the Lifespan

Reaction time (RT) follows an inverted-U trajectory over a lifetime. It improves rapidly through childhood and adolescence as the brain's white matter myelinates and inhibitory circuits mature. Peak performance is reached in the late teens to mid-twenties, then begins a slow, steady lengthening that continues through later adulthood. By age 75, the average person is roughly 50–80ms slower than their personal peak — a difference that is modest in most everyday contexts but meaningful in high-speed activities like driving or sports.

These figures reflect simple visual reaction time: you see a single stimulus and press a button as quickly as possible. Choice reaction time (selecting among multiple options) follows the same lifespan arc but is consistently 100–200ms slower at every age. Both metrics are useful; together they paint a fuller picture of neural processing speed.

Why Reaction Time Slows With Age

Age-related RT slowing has two primary neural drivers:

Neural conduction velocity. Signals travel along axons wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath. From around age 40, myelin integrity gradually deteriorates, reducing conduction speed. A signal that once crossed a long motor pathway in 15ms may take 18–20ms in a 65-year-old — a small change that compounds across the many synapses in a reaction pathway.

Processing resource depletion. Older adults allocate cognitive resources less efficiently. Prefrontal cortex volume, dopaminergic signalling, and working-memory capacity all decline modestly with age, meaning that the "decision" component of even a simple RT task takes fractionally longer to complete. This resource-based slowing is distinct from pure conduction speed and explains why complexity matters more to older adults — adding just one response option widens the RT gap between young and older adults considerably.

Importantly, intra-individual variability — the degree to which your RT fluctuates from trial to trial — also increases with age and is considered by some researchers to be a more sensitive marker of neural aging than mean RT alone.

Factors Affecting Reaction Time Beyond Age

Age explains a substantial portion of RT variance, but far from all of it. The following modifiable and non-modifiable factors can shift your RT up or down by 20–50ms regardless of your age group:

  • Practice and training: Even 10–15 sessions of targeted RT training can reduce mean RT by 15–30ms. The effect is largest in untrained individuals.
  • Aerobic fitness: Cardiovascularly fit adults consistently outperform sedentary peers by 20–40ms across all adult age groups. Exercise improves cerebral blood flow and supports white matter health.
  • Sleep: A single night of poor sleep (under 6 hours) can slow RT by 20–50ms — comparable to mild alcohol intoxication. Chronic sleep restriction produces larger and more durable deficits.
  • Caffeine: Moderate caffeine (100–200mg) reliably reduces RT by 10–20ms for 2–4 hours, particularly in fatigued or sleep-deprived individuals.
  • Alcohol and sedatives: Even sub-legal blood alcohol levels (0.04–0.07%) slow RT by 40–100ms and increase variability substantially.
  • Time of day: RT is typically fastest in the late morning to early afternoon, aligning with the circadian peak in core body temperature and alertness.
  • Stimulus modality: Auditory stimuli produce RTs roughly 20–40ms faster than visual stimuli at all ages because auditory processing has shorter neural pathways to the motor cortex.

How to Measure Your Reaction Time

The most reliable way to assess your RT is through a standardised computerised test. Browser-based tests can achieve millisecond accuracy when the display refresh rate is accounted for. For meaningful results, take at least 20 trials, discard obvious outliers (anticipatory responses under 150ms or distracted responses over 600ms), and average the remainder.

Research Insight: Regular reaction time training can partially offset age-related slowing. A 2013 meta-analysis found that cognitively demanding tasks with a speed component produced the largest and most durable RT improvements across adult age groups — larger than simple RT practice alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • What is a good reaction time for my age?

    It depends on your age group. For adults 18–34, under 250ms is excellent. Ages 35–54 should aim for under 300ms for a "very good" rating. Adults 55–74 typically score 300–380ms, which is considered average for their age. Always compare yourself to age-matched norms rather than young-adult benchmarks — the goal is to be fast for your age, not to match 20-year-olds.

  • At what age is reaction time fastest?

    Simple visual reaction time peaks between ages 18 and 24, with average scores in the 240–270ms range. Some studies place the absolute peak closer to age 22. After 25, reaction time gradually lengthens by roughly 1–2ms per year under typical conditions, though highly trained individuals can maintain near-peak performance well into their 30s.

  • Can you improve reaction time as you age?

    Yes. Research shows that targeted reaction time training, aerobic exercise, and adequate sleep can measurably reduce reaction times even in older adults. Studies of older adults who engage in regular speed-based cognitive training show RT improvements of 15–30ms sustained over months. While training cannot fully reverse age-related neural slowing, regular practice keeps you in the faster end of your age-group norms.
