About Processing Speed Training

Processing speed (Gs in the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model of intelligence) refers to the ability to automatically and fluently perform simple cognitive tasks, especially when under pressure to maintain focused attention and concentration. It is one of the most reliably measured components of the g factor — general intelligence — and one of the strongest predictors of performance in cognitively demanding occupations and educational settings.

At the neural level, processing speed is closely linked to the efficiency of white matter — the myelinated axonal pathways that connect distant brain regions. Faster neural conduction velocity across these pathways correlates with higher processing speed scores. This is why processing speed is particularly sensitive to neurological conditions that affect white matter, including multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, and the normal aging process.

The Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), designed by Aaron Smith in 1973, remains the gold standard measure of processing speed in both clinical neuropsychology and large-scale aging research. The task is elegantly simple: given a key pairing nine symbols with the digits 1–9, participants fill in as many digits as possible in 90 seconds. The score is the number of correct substitutions — a direct measure of perceptual speed, visual scanning, and motor output rate. The games on BrainForge adapt this paradigm for progressive training across 30 levels.

Age-related changes in processing speed are well documented. Cross-sectional studies show a reliable decline beginning in the mid-30s, with the slope steepening from the 50s onwards. Timothy Salthouse's processing speed theory of cognitive aging argues that this speed reduction is the primary driver of age-related declines in working memory and fluid reasoning — because if information is processed more slowly, it may decay before it can be fully used. Critically, the brain retains neuroplasticity throughout life, and processing speed training has been shown to slow — though not reverse — age-related decline.

The ACTIVE study, a landmark randomised controlled trial tracking over 2,800 older adults across 10 years, found that processing speed training (using computerised speed-of-processing tasks) produced significant transfer to real-world driving performance and reduced at-fault crash risk by approximately 40% compared to a control group — one of the strongest demonstrations of far-transfer effects from cognitive training in the literature.

How to Improve Your Processing Speed

  • Minimise deliberation time. Processing speed games reward fast, fluent responding. The goal is to make the stimulus-response mapping so automatic that it no longer requires conscious deliberation. This automaticity comes only with repetition — the more trials you complete, the more the mapping becomes a fast neural shortcut rather than a slow inferential step.
  • Increase aerobic fitness. A meta-analysis by Colcombe and Kramer (2003) found that aerobic exercise produced particularly large improvements in processing speed tasks compared to non-aerobic activities. Even 20 minutes of moderate-intensity cardio before a training session measurably improves reaction time and decision speed.
  • Reduce cognitive fatigue. Processing speed is extremely sensitive to fatigue states. Training when alert produces the clearest signal and the strongest adaptation. Scheduling sessions in the morning, after caffeine if habitual, and away from meals tends to yield the best performance.
  • Work the speed–accuracy trade-off deliberately. In the beginning, prioritise accuracy over raw speed to build correct automatized mappings. Once accuracy exceeds 90%, begin deliberately pushing response speed even at the cost of some errors — this is how the brain is forced to build faster pathways rather than slow deliberate ones.
  • Combine training types. Processing speed is composed of perceptual encoding speed, decision speed, and motor execution speed. The games here target all three. Supplementing game training with activities that require rapid sensorimotor integration — such as tennis, table tennis, or rhythm-based video games — may further accelerate gains.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is processing speed the same as reaction time?

They are closely related but not identical. Reaction time (RT) is the time from stimulus onset to response initiation — it captures the full input-to-output latency chain including perceptual encoding, decision, and motor initiation. Processing speed, as measured by tests like the SDMT, is a broader construct that emphasizes the rate of accurate information processing, not just latency. Simple RT tasks measure a single stimulus-response cycle; processing speed tasks typically involve sustained throughput across many trials under timed conditions. High processing speed generally predicts fast RT, but someone can have fast simple RT yet show reduced processing speed on more complex scanning tasks due to differences in visual search or semantic access.

Can processing speed decline be reversed?

Current evidence suggests that training can slow the decline and improve performance on trained and related tasks, but wholesale reversal of age-related processing speed losses is not consistently demonstrated. The ACTIVE study showed durable benefits 10 years after training, but primarily as a reduction in the rate of further decline rather than a return to young-adult levels. For younger adults and those experiencing speed deficits due to treatable factors (poor sleep, depression, sedentary lifestyle, cognitive overload), significant improvements are achievable through training combined with lifestyle changes.

How does processing speed relate to intelligence?

Processing speed is one of the most reliably measured components of the g factor (general intelligence). In the CHC model of intelligence, Gs (cognitive processing speed) is one of roughly 10 broad abilities, alongside fluid reasoning (Gf), crystallised intelligence (Gc), and working memory capacity (Gwm). Arthur Jensen's work on inspection time and choice reaction time showed that even millisecond-scale differences in neural processing speed predict IQ scores measured by conventional tests. However, processing speed is neither necessary nor sufficient for high intelligence — many highly intelligent people with slower baseline speed compensate through excellent strategy use, broad knowledge bases, and efficient working memory management.
