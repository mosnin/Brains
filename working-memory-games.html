About Working Memory Training

Working memory (WM) is the cognitive system that temporarily holds and actively manipulates a limited amount of information. Unlike long-term memory, which stores knowledge over days and years, working memory operates in the present moment — it is what keeps the beginning of a sentence in mind while you read to the end, allows a chef to track multiple orders simultaneously, and lets a student hold one number while calculating another.

The most influential model of working memory was proposed by Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch in 1974 and has since been refined with the addition of a fourth component. The model consists of:

  • Phonological Loop — handles verbal and acoustic information. It has a phonological store that holds sound-based items for about two seconds, and an articulatory rehearsal process (like an inner voice) that refreshes them. Digit span and letter span tasks target this component directly.
  • Visuospatial Sketchpad — maintains and manipulates visual and spatial information, such as the layout of a room or the face of a person you just met. Corsi blocks and visual pattern tasks exercise this subsystem.
  • Central Executive — the supervisory attentional system that coordinates the two slave systems, focuses attention, and switches between tasks. Memory updating and dual-task paradigms like Dual N-Back stress the central executive most heavily.
  • Episodic Buffer — added by Baddeley in 2000, this component integrates information from the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and long-term memory into coherent multimodal representations.

Working memory capacity is one of the strongest predictors of academic achievement, reading comprehension, mathematical ability, and fluid intelligence (Gf). Children and adults with higher WM spans consistently outperform those with lower spans on complex reasoning tasks, even when raw IQ scores are controlled for.

The research on working memory training is both exciting and nuanced. Studies by Jaeggi and colleagues (2008, 2011) showed that Dual N-Back training produced gains in fluid intelligence — a finding that attracted enormous scientific interest. Subsequent meta-analyses (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Soveri et al., 2017) found robust near-transfer effects: WM training reliably improves performance on untrained WM tasks. Far-transfer to broader cognitive abilities remains debated, but consistent practice is associated with real-world benefits such as reduced mind-wandering and improved classroom attention in children with ADHD.

How to Improve Your Working Memory

Improving working memory is about more than playing games. To get the most from training, follow these evidence-based principles:

  • Practice adaptively. The games on BrainForge increase in difficulty as your performance improves. This adaptive challenge — always working at the edge of your ability — is the key feature that separates effective training from mere task practice.
  • Be consistent, not intensive. Research points to 15–20 minutes per day, five days a week, as the optimal training dose. Daily consistency matters far more than occasional marathon sessions.
  • Train multiple components. Because the phonological loop and visuospatial sketchpad are partially independent, mixing verbal tasks (digit/letter span) with spatial tasks (Corsi, Spatial N-Back) produces broader gains than focusing on a single game.
  • Sleep and exercise. Sleep consolidates memory traces — pulling an all-nighter before a cognitively demanding day dramatically reduces WM capacity. Aerobic exercise reliably boosts BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a protein associated with stronger synaptic connections in prefrontal and hippocampal circuits that support working memory.
  • Reduce cognitive load from distractions. Multitasking and constant interruptions fragment attention and tax working memory unnecessarily. Dedicated focus time, free from phone notifications, effectively frees up WM resources for the task at hand.
  • Use chunking and mnemonics. Real-world WM is supported by strategies. Grouping digits into chunks (723-456 rather than 7-2-3-4-5-6) or turning a sequence into a word or story exploits long-term memory to offload the phonological loop, leaving resources available for processing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal working memory span?

The classic figure from George Miller's 1956 paper is "7 ± 2" items — meaning most adults can hold 5–9 digits in a simple forward span task. More recent work by Nelson Cowan (2001) suggests the true capacity of working memory, when rehearsal is prevented, is closer to 4 ± 1 chunks. Visuospatial spans measured by the Corsi Block test average around 5–6 positions for healthy adults. These numbers decline modestly but measurably from middle age onwards, making regular training especially valuable as you get older.

Does Dual N-Back actually increase IQ?

The 2008 Jaeggi et al. study in PNAS reported that Dual N-Back training produced dose-dependent gains in a matrix reasoning measure of fluid intelligence (Gf). This sparked enormous interest because Gf had long been considered largely fixed. The results have been partially replicated but not universally so, and meta-analyses present a mixed picture. The current scientific consensus is that WM training reliably improves WM task performance (near transfer) and may produce modest gains in closely related attention and reasoning tasks, but dramatic IQ increases should not be expected. That said, improved WM is itself valuable — it supports better learning, reasoning, and daily cognitive functioning.

How long before I see improvement?

Most people notice meaningful improvement on the training tasks themselves within 1–2 weeks of daily practice, as the brain adapts its strategies and neural efficiency in the targeted circuits increases. Real-world functional benefits — such as finding it easier to follow complex instructions or retain a phone number — typically become noticeable after 3–4 weeks of consistent training. Research studies typically use 20–25 sessions of training, so committing to about a month of daily 15-minute sessions is a reasonable minimum before evaluating progress.
