Reaction Time

Average Reaction Time: What Is Normal?

Simple RT averages 200–250 ms for young adults. Learn what average reaction time is, how it's measured, and where you stand.

What Is Reaction Time?

Reaction time (RT) is the interval between the onset of a stimulus and the initiation of a motor response. It is one of the oldest and most reliable measures in experimental psychology, first studied systematically in the nineteenth century as a window into the speed of mental processes.

Psychologists distinguish between several types of reaction time tasks, each measuring slightly different cognitive processes:

  • Simple reaction time (SRT) — one stimulus, one response. A light appears; you press a button. This measures the pure speed of detecting a signal and generating a motor response.
  • Choice reaction time (CRT) — multiple stimuli, each requiring a different response. Two or four lights appear in different locations; you press the corresponding button. This adds decision time to the equation.
  • Discrimination reaction time — you respond only to a target stimulus and withhold the response to non-target stimuli, measuring selective attention and inhibitory control.

Hick's Law: Why Choice RT Is Slower

The relationship between the number of stimulus-response alternatives and reaction time was quantified by W.E. Hick in 1952. Hick's Law states that RT increases linearly with the logarithm (base 2) of the number of alternatives. In simple terms, each doubling of the number of choices adds a roughly constant increment of time — typically around 100–150 ms per bit of information in healthy young adults.

This is why a four-choice reaction time (roughly 500 ms average) is meaningfully slower than a two-choice task (roughly 400 ms), which is itself slower than simple RT (roughly 250 ms). The slope of this function — how much each added choice slows you down — is itself related to cognitive processing capacity and is used in clinical assessment.

Visual vs Auditory vs Tactile Reaction Time

Reaction time differs systematically across sensory modalities. Auditory stimuli produce the fastest simple reactions, averaging around 150–160 ms, because auditory signals travel faster through peripheral nerves and require less cortical processing before a motor response can be initiated. Visual stimuli average 200–250 ms, and tactile stimuli fall between the two.

The modality difference has practical implications. In sports that use audio signals (swimming, sprinting), athletes must respond to a sound rather than a visual cue, exploiting the auditory speed advantage. Tactile stimuli — for instance, a vibrating device — fall between visual and auditory in terms of typical RT.

What Is Actually Being Measured?

A reaction time measurement captures the sum of several distinct processes, each contributing a portion of the total latency:

  • Stimulus detection — sensory receptors transduce the physical stimulus (light, sound) into neural signals. This takes roughly 10–30 ms depending on modality and intensity.
  • Neural conduction — signals travel from peripheral receptors to primary sensory cortex. Myelination of nerve fibers is a key determinant of conduction speed.
  • Cortical processing — the brain identifies the stimulus, matches it to a response rule, and prepares the motor command. This is the largest and most variable component, taking 100–200 ms in simple tasks and much longer in complex ones.
  • Motor output — the motor command travels from motor cortex to the muscles, which then contract and move the effector (hand, foot). This contributes roughly 50–100 ms.

In practice, online reaction time tests measure from the moment the stimulus appears on screen to the moment a keypress is registered. This sum captures all of the above components, plus any display latency and input device latency. Well-designed online tools minimize these hardware contributions and report the true neural-motor RT with acceptable precision.

Factors That Affect Reaction Time

Age

Simple RT is fastest in early adulthood, typically peaking around ages 22–25. Thereafter, RT lengthens gradually at roughly 1–2 ms per year in healthy individuals. By age 65, simple RT has increased by roughly 25–30% compared to the mid-twenties peak. This slowing is primarily attributed to reduced cortical processing speed and slower neural conduction due to age-related myelin changes.

Caffeine

Caffeine (100–300 mg) reliably reduces simple and choice RT by approximately 10–30 ms compared to placebo. The mechanism involves adenosine receptor antagonism in the brain, increasing arousal and facilitating neurotransmission. The effect is most pronounced when the individual is caffeine-naive, sleep-deprived, or has withdrawn from caffeine use.

Fatigue and Sleep Deprivation

Even partial sleep restriction (6 hours per night for one week) produces RT deficits comparable to 24 hours of total sleep deprivation. Fatigue degrades not only average RT speed but also response variability — fatigued individuals show more "lapses" (very slow responses exceeding 500 ms) even when most responses remain fast. This variability is a particularly sensitive marker of impaired vigilance.

Practice and Warm-Up

RT shows rapid improvement across the first 10–20 practice trials as participants adapt to the task structure and reduce procedural uncertainty. Beyond this initial learning, additional practice over weeks produces more modest but real gains, particularly in choice RT tasks where consistent mapping of stimuli to responses allows automatization.

Stress and Arousal

Moderate increases in arousal (the inverted-U Yerkes-Dodson relationship) can slightly accelerate RT, while extreme stress degrades it. Competitive athletes often train specifically to maintain rapid, accurate responses under high-arousal conditions.

Research Note

Galton (1884) was the first to systematically measure human reaction time at scale, testing over 9,000 visitors to his Anthropometric Laboratory. Modern studies consistently find simple RT averages 220–250 ms in young adults, with auditory RT approximately 30–40 ms faster than visual RT across populations.

How to Test Your Reaction Time

The most reliable way to measure your own RT is to use a properly designed, low-latency online test under controlled conditions: sit comfortably, use a wired keyboard or mouse, ensure the screen is not lagging, avoid distractions, and complete at least 20 trials to obtain a stable average. Your median (not mean) RT is the most informative single statistic, as it is less distorted by occasional slow outliers due to momentary inattention.

Practical Tip

Take your test in the morning after a full night's sleep and at least one cup of water. Avoid testing immediately after exercise (temporarily elevated HR can speed RT) or in the late afternoon slump. Five blocks of 10 trials each, discarding the first block as a warm-up, gives a reliable baseline.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a fast reaction time?

A reaction time below 200 ms is considered fast and places you in the top 15% of the population. Elite athletes and trained individuals often achieve 160–190 ms on simple reaction time tasks. Anything under 150 ms on a visual stimulus is exceptional and may indicate either very high neural conduction speed or a slight anticipation of the signal.

How is reaction time measured?

Reaction time is measured as the interval between a stimulus onset and the beginning of a motor response. In lab settings, high-speed chronometers or computer-based tasks record this interval in milliseconds. Online tests use the browser's performance API to timestamp the stimulus and the keypress or click response, typically achieving accuracy within 10–20 ms.

Does reaction time predict intelligence?

Yes, weakly. Meta-analyses report correlations of approximately r = 0.20–0.30 between simple reaction time speed (and variability) and general intelligence (g). Choice reaction time shows a slightly stronger correlation. The relationship is thought to reflect a shared underlying factor of neural processing efficiency, but RT alone is far from a reliable intelligence measure.
