What Is Reaction Time?

Reaction time (RT) is the elapsed time between the onset of a stimulus and the initiation of a motor response. It is one of the oldest and most studied measures in all of experimental psychology — researchers have been measuring it since the 1860s, when astronomer Friedrich Bessel noticed that different observers recorded star-crossing times differently due to individual differences in reaction speed.

Reaction time is not simply "reflexes." It reflects the speed of neural conduction across multiple processing stages: stimulus detection, sensory encoding, perceptual processing, decision-making, motor command generation, and muscle activation. Each stage adds milliseconds to the total, making RT a sensitive composite measure of overall neural processing efficiency.

For simple reaction time — where only one stimulus appears and only one response is required — the average healthy adult responds in approximately 250 milliseconds. For choice reaction time — where different stimuli require different responses — average RT extends to roughly 350–400 milliseconds, because the brain must identify the stimulus and select the correct response before acting.

Average Reaction Times by Type

  • Simple Reaction Time: ~250 ms (healthy adults)
  • Choice Reaction Time: ~350–400 ms (2 choices)
  • Stop-Signal RT: ~200 ms (the time to inhibit a response)

Types of Reaction Time

Simple Reaction Time

In a simple RT task, one stimulus appears and one response is required — click when you see the target. This measures the minimum time for the complete sensorimotor processing chain. Simple RT is the purest measure of neural processing speed and is used as a baseline in most cognitive research.

Choice Reaction Time

In a choice RT task, multiple stimuli appear and each requires a different response. The additional processing required to identify the stimulus and select the appropriate response adds 100–150 ms compared to simple RT. The relationship between number of choices and RT is described by Hick's Law: RT increases logarithmically with the number of possible stimulus-response pairs.

Try it: Choice Reaction Time Test →

Stop-Signal Reaction Time

In a stop-signal task, the participant must initiate a response to a go stimulus, but occasionally a stop signal appears and the response must be cancelled. The stop-signal reaction time (SSRT) is estimated from the race between the go process and the stop process — typically around 200 ms. SSRT is a key measure of inhibitory control and response suppression speed.

What Affects Reaction Time?

Age

Reaction time is fastest in the late teens and early twenties, then gradually slows across the lifespan. The slowing is due to multiple factors: reduced neural conduction velocity, slower synaptic transmission, and changes in the speed of perceptual processing. Decline accelerates after age 60, though regular physical and cognitive exercise can meaningfully slow this trajectory.

Fatigue and Sleep

Sleep deprivation is one of the strongest drivers of RT slowing. A single night of poor sleep can add 20–50 ms to average RT. Sustained wakefulness produces RT profiles indistinguishable from mild alcohol intoxication. Testing RT at consistent times of day — when well-rested — gives the most reliable baseline.

Caffeine

Caffeine is one of the most reliably RT-improving substances. Doses of 100–300 mg typically reduce simple RT by 10–30 ms and improve sustained attention. The effect peaks 30–60 minutes after consumption and lasts 3–5 hours.

Practice and Training

Reaction time improves with practice, particularly for tasks involving the specific stimulus-response mappings being trained. Simple RT shows relatively modest practice effects after the first few sessions, while choice RT continues to improve as stimulus-response associations become automatized. Regular training on BrainForge reaction time games produces measurable practice-related improvements within 2–4 weeks.

Stimulus Modality

Humans respond fastest to auditory stimuli (~150 ms) compared to visual (~200 ms) and tactile (~150 ms) stimuli. This is why starting guns are used in sprinting rather than visual signals — auditory RT is approximately 50 ms faster.

Stimulus Intensity and Probability

More intense stimuli produce faster RT (Pieron's Law). Stimuli that are more probable also produce faster RT, because the nervous system maintains higher preparatory activation for expected stimuli. This is why foreperiod manipulation — varying the time between a warning signal and the target — is used in research to control anticipatory effects.

Average Reaction Time by Age

Reaction time follows a reliable developmental and aging trajectory across the lifespan. The values below are approximate averages for simple visual reaction time based on population studies:

How to Improve Your Reaction Time

1. Regular Cognitive Training

Targeted practice on reaction time tasks produces measurable improvements, particularly in the early stages of training. BrainForge's Simple Reaction Time and Choice Reaction Time games use adaptive difficulty to keep you in the optimal challenge zone. Daily 10–15 minute sessions show benefits within 2–4 weeks.

2. Aerobic Exercise

A robust body of research shows that regular aerobic exercise is one of the most effective interventions for improving reaction time across all ages. A single bout of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise can reduce RT by 15–30 ms for up to 90 minutes afterward. Chronic exercise produces longer-lasting structural benefits through increased cerebral blood flow, neurogenesis in the hippocampus, and improved white matter integrity.

3. Optimize Sleep

Sleep is when the brain consolidates motor and cognitive learning. Consistently getting 7–9 hours of quality sleep is one of the most powerful reaction time improvements available — it is also one of the most neglected. Tracking your RT at the same time each day reveals the sensitivity of your nervous system to sleep quality.

4. Sport and Action Video Games

Action video game players consistently demonstrate faster RT than non-players, with differences of 50–100 ms in some studies. The key mechanism is probabilistic inference — experienced action gamers learn to optimally integrate noisy sensory evidence over time. Contact sports, racket sports, and martial arts all produce similar RT improvements through sport-specific perceptual-motor training.

5. Minimize Distraction During Tests

RT is highly sensitive to distraction, divided attention, and dual-task demands. To get an accurate baseline, test in a quiet environment, use a mouse rather than a touchscreen where possible (touchscreen input adds ~30–50 ms of hardware latency), and complete 5–10 trials before taking the average.
