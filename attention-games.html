About Attention Training

Attention is arguably the most fundamental cognitive function. Without the ability to select relevant stimuli from the flood of sensory information arriving every second, higher cognitive processes like memory encoding, reasoning, and decision-making would be impossible. Cognitive scientists distinguish several largely separable attention types, each supported by different neural networks:

  • Sustained attention (vigilance) — the ability to maintain alertness and detect rare or infrequent targets over an extended period. Supported by the right hemisphere's frontoparietal network and highly sensitive to fatigue and sleep deprivation. The vigilance decrement — a drop in hit rate after roughly 30 minutes on a monotonous detection task — is one of the most robust findings in experimental psychology (Mackworth, 1948).
  • Selective attention — the ability to focus on relevant stimuli while filtering out competing distractors. The Flanker task and the Stroop color-word test are the two most widely used laboratory measures of selective attention and conflict monitoring. Neural underpinnings include the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC).
  • Divided attention — the ability to simultaneously process information from two or more sources. Dual-task paradigms such as Dual N-Back place heavy demands on divided attention, revealing capacity limits in the central executive.
  • Executive attention — the highest-level attention control, responsible for resolving conflicts between competing response tendencies, overriding habitual responses, and maintaining task-relevant goals in the face of distraction. Closely related to executive function and supported primarily by prefrontal circuits.

Why does attention training matter beyond the laboratory? Attention deficits are central to many common conditions including ADHD, anxiety disorders, traumatic brain injury, and age-related cognitive decline. Even in healthy individuals, chronic stress and excessive smartphone use have been associated with reductions in sustained attention span and increases in mind-wandering. Targeted training using validated paradigms offers a structured, evidence-based approach to rebuilding attentional control.

Research on the Continuous Performance Test (CPT) and its variants shows that performance on the task is highly sensitive to ADHD, sleep deprivation, and psychostimulant medication effects — making it both a clinical diagnostic tool and a sensitive measure of attentional capacity. The Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART), developed by Robertson et al. (1997), is particularly useful for detecting lapses of attention and mind-wandering episodes, which are associated with poorer academic and occupational outcomes.

How to Improve Your Attention and Focus

Improving attention is not purely about willpower — it requires deliberate training combined with lifestyle factors that support the attentional networks of the brain:

  • Practice consistently with adaptive difficulty. Games that stay just beyond your current ability force the prefrontal and parietal networks to work harder, driving neuroplastic change. Playing at an easy level yields performance improvements but not the neural adaptation needed for real-world transfer.
  • Train during your peak alertness window. Individual chronotypes vary, but most people show peak attentional capacity in the mid-morning. Training at this time leverages natural arousal rhythms, while training when tired simply documents your attentional failures rather than training you to avoid them.
  • Minimise digital distraction during sessions. Switching between a training task and a phone notification activates entirely different neural circuits. Each interruption incurs a "switch cost" that takes 20–30 seconds to recover from. Full-screen, notification-free training sessions produce better outcomes.
  • Prioritise sleep. Even a single night of poor sleep (less than 6 hours) produces attention deficits equivalent to two nights of total sleep deprivation in laboratory measures. No amount of training compensates for chronic sleep deprivation.
  • Practise mindfulness meditation. Multiple randomised controlled trials (e.g., Tang et al., 2007; Jha et al., 2010) show that as little as 5–10 days of mindfulness training significantly improves performance on sustained and selective attention tasks. Meditation and cognitive training appear to target complementary aspects of attentional control and may work synergistically.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between attention and concentration?

In everyday language the words are often used interchangeably, but in cognitive psychology they refer to related but distinct processes. Attention is broader — it encompasses the initial selection of relevant stimuli from the environment, the filtering of distractors, and the general alertness state of the organism. Concentration (or sustained attention) is a specific subtype: the ability to maintain attentional focus on a task over time without it drifting. You can have good attention (quick, accurate stimulus detection) but poor concentration (difficulty staying on task). The games on BrainForge train both aspects.

Can these games help with ADHD?

Attention training using CPT-style tasks and Go/No-Go tasks is used clinically as both an assessment and an adjunct intervention for ADHD. Meta-analyses of computerised cognitive training in children with ADHD (Cortese et al., 2015) show statistically significant near-transfer effects on attention and inhibition measures. However, the evidence for far-transfer to classroom behaviour and academic performance is more limited. These games should be seen as a supportive tool alongside professional clinical management, not as a standalone treatment. Always consult a qualified healthcare professional for clinical concerns.

How long should I train attention each day?

Research protocols typically use sessions of 15–25 minutes for attention training. Longer sessions risk fatigue-induced performance degradation that works against skill development. The vigilance decrement shows that even well-rested adults begin losing accuracy on sustained tasks after 20–30 minutes, so breaking training into two 10-minute blocks with a short break may actually produce better results than a single 20-minute block. Daily or near-daily practice (5 days a week) over at least 4–6 weeks is the minimum needed to assess meaningful change.
