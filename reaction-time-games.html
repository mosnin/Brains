About Reaction Time Training

Reaction time (RT) is one of the oldest and most extensively studied measures in experimental psychology, dating to Francis Galton's anthropometric laboratory at the International Health Exhibition of 1884, where he measured the reaction times of over 9,000 members of the public. Despite over 140 years of research, RT remains a fundamental window into nervous system function — and one of the most sensitive measures of cognitive aging, fatigue, and mental health.

Researchers distinguish several types of reaction time depending on task complexity:

  • Simple reaction time (SRT) — one stimulus, one response. The participant waits for a single signal and responds as fast as possible. Average SRT for healthy young adults is approximately 200–250 ms for visual stimuli and 150–200 ms for auditory stimuli. This apparently simple task has surprisingly high test-retest reliability and correlates moderately with general intelligence (Jensen, 1987).
  • Choice reaction time (CRT) — multiple stimuli, each requiring a different response. CRT is predictably longer than SRT because the brain must discriminate the stimulus identity before selecting the correct response. William Edmund Hick (1952) and Ray Hyman (1953) independently established what is now called Hick's Law: CRT increases as a linear function of the logarithm of the number of stimulus-response alternatives. Each doubling of alternatives adds roughly 150 ms — a remarkably orderly relationship that has been replicated thousands of times.
  • Stop-signal reaction time (SSRT) — estimated from the Stop Signal paradigm. On most trials the participant responds to a directional arrow; on occasional trials a stop signal (auditory beep or visual change) appears after the Go signal, requiring the participant to cancel the response. SSRT is the latency of the internal inhibition process — an unobservable "race" between the Go process and the Stop process. Average SSRT for healthy adults is approximately 200–250 ms. SSRT is substantially longer in individuals with ADHD, addiction disorders, and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Reaction time is sensitive to a wide range of biological and psychological factors. Caffeine reliably reduces SRT by 20–40 ms in habitual consumers. A single night of sleep restriction to 6 hours produces RT increases comparable to 0.05% blood alcohol concentration. Age effects are well-documented: mean SRT increases from approximately 240 ms at age 20 to 280 ms at age 40, 320 ms at age 60, and 380 ms at age 80 — with increasing variability (intraindividual variability in RT is itself an important predictor of cognitive status in aging research).

Arthur Jensen's work in the 1980s established a reliable negative correlation between RT and IQ (faster = smarter, on average), with the RT-IQ correlation being stronger for CRT than SRT. This relationship is thought to reflect the efficiency of neural information processing — the same underlying substrate that supports both fast physical responses and higher-order reasoning. Intraindividual variability (the standard deviation of RT across trials) often predicts cognitive ability better than mean RT alone.

How to Improve Your Reaction Time

  • Practice the stimulus-response pairing. RT improvement comes largely from reducing the time taken to identify the stimulus and select the correct response, not from speeding up motor execution (which is already close to physiological minimum). Repetition builds faster, more automatic neural pathways for the specific stimulus-response mapping you train.
  • Stay well-rested and alert. RT is among the cognitive measures most sensitive to sleep deprivation. A single night of poor sleep can add 30–60 ms to mean SRT. Training when fatigued documents your impaired state rather than building skill.
  • Use caffeine strategically. If you are a habitual caffeine consumer, having your normal dose before a training session leverages its well-documented arousal-enhancing effects on RT. Novel users should avoid caffeine as it can increase response variability in non-habituated individuals.
  • Add physical reaction training. Sports that require rapid stimulus discrimination — racket sports, martial arts, ball sports — provide high volumes of natural reaction time training in ecologically valid contexts and may enhance RT transfer to laboratory measures more broadly than game training alone.
  • Maintain good cardiovascular fitness. Meta-analyses show that cardiorespiratory fitness correlates negatively with RT across the lifespan, with better-fit adults showing faster and more consistent RTs. This is thought to reflect improved cerebrovascular efficiency and higher baseline dopaminergic tone in motor and prefrontal circuits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good reaction time for my age?

For simple visual reaction time, typical benchmarks are approximately 220–250 ms for young adults (18–25), 240–270 ms for adults aged 25–40, 270–310 ms for adults aged 40–60, and 320–380 ms for adults over 60. These figures are for laboratory conditions with optimal preparation — real-world RT in more complex situations (driving, sports) is somewhat longer. Bear in mind that the standard deviation across trials is as important as the mean: highly consistent fast responses are more useful than occasional very fast responses punctuated by attention lapses. Web-based tests like the ones on BrainForge add a small amount of input latency compared to laboratory equipment, so your in-game times may be 20–30 ms slower than equivalent lab measurements.

Can you actually train reaction time or is it fixed?

Reaction time has a substantial genetic and neurological component — the fundamental conduction velocity of your myelinated axons is not easily changed. However, the choice and stop components of RT are strongly influenced by practice. CRT improvements of 30–60 ms are commonly seen after weeks of training, primarily through faster stimulus identification and automatized response selection. Simple RT also improves with practice, but the gains plateau sooner (within 5–10 sessions) because baseline SRT is already close to the neurophysiological floor. The most meaningful improvements from sustained training come from greater consistency (lower RT variability), faster stop processes (lower SSRT), and better utilisation of preparatory attention — all of which are genuinely trainable.

Does faster reaction time mean higher intelligence?

On average, yes — the correlation between mean RT and IQ is reliably negative (faster RT, higher IQ) across large samples. The correlation is modest for simple RT (r ≈ -0.2 to -0.3) but stronger for choice RT and for RT variability (r ≈ -0.4 to -0.5). Jensen interpreted this as reflecting the speed and efficiency of neural information processing underlying both fast responses and abstract reasoning. However, individual exceptions are common — high IQ is neither necessary nor sufficient for fast RT, and vice versa. RT measures a narrow slice of cognitive function. Extraordinary RT is most important in time-critical domains like elite sport, emergency response, and certain driving situations; in most academic and intellectual tasks, deliberative reasoning capacity matters far more than raw neural latency.
