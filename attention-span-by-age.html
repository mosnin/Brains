Attention Span by Age: Research-Based Norms

Attention span is one of the most misunderstood concepts in cognitive science. Despite viral claims, humans have not developed goldfish-level attention. Here is what decades of lab research actually shows about how long people can focus at different ages — and what that means for you.

The "8-Second Attention Span" Myth — Debunked

In 2015, a widely-shared statistic claimed that the average human attention span had dropped to 8 seconds — shorter than a goldfish. The claim was attributed to a Microsoft Canada report on media habits. The problem: the study never measured sustained attentional capacity at all. It surveyed how quickly people switched between digital media screens, which is a measure of attention switching frequency, not the maximum duration of focused attention on a task.

Neuroscientist Gemma Briggs, speaking to the BBC, pointed out that "attention span" varies entirely based on the task and individual motivation. A person who appears to have a short attention span for a dull lecture may maintain intense focus for two hours on a video game, a piece of music, or a problem they genuinely care about. The concept of a single fixed "attention span" number is not scientifically meaningful.

What research does measure is sustained attention: the ability to maintain performance on a demanding vigilance task over time. These laboratory norms tell a more nuanced and accurate story.

The data below combines findings from continuous performance tests (CPT), sustained attention to response tasks (SART), and vigilance research. How Attention Develops in Children

Sustained attention capacity grows substantially through childhood and adolescence, driven by the protracted development of the prefrontal cortex and its connections to the anterior cingulate cortex — the brain's core "attention network." Children aged 5–8 can sustain attention for only 5–10 minutes before performance degrades, not because they lack motivation, but because the neural infrastructure for sustained focus is still being built.

A practical rule of thumb — sometimes cited in educational psychology — is that a child can concentrate for roughly 2–5 minutes per year of age on a given task before needing a break. A 6-year-old: 12–30 minutes. A 10-year-old: 20–50 minutes. These estimates align with the laboratory data above and have implications for classroom design and homework scheduling.

By age 13–17, adolescents approach (but do not quite reach) adult-level sustained attention capacity in laboratory settings. Their performance is particularly sensitive to motivational context — they can sustain attention far longer on self-chosen tasks than on externally imposed ones, which is a feature of adolescent cognitive development, not a deficit.

Myths vs. Reality About Attention Span

  • Myth: "Smartphones have permanently shortened human attention spans."
    Reality: There is no robust longitudinal evidence that device use has caused population-level declines in sustained attentional capacity. What has changed is how people choose to allocate attention, not the underlying capacity.
  • Myth: "Multitasking improves attention."
    Reality: True cognitive multitasking does not exist. What people call multitasking is rapid task-switching, which consistently reduces performance on both tasks and increases error rates.
  • Myth: "Some people are naturally good at multitasking."
    Reality: Studies by Ophir et al. (2009) found that self-described heavy media multitaskers performed worse on attentional control tasks than light multitaskers, not better.

ADHD and Attention Across the Lifespan

ADHD is characterised by clinically significant difficulties with sustained attention, impulse control, and executive regulation. On continuous performance tests, individuals with ADHD typically show higher error rates (both omissions and commissions), greater intra-individual variability in response times, and steeper vigilance decrements over time — even when mean scores are not dramatically different from controls.

Importantly, ADHD is not simply "low attention span." Many individuals with ADHD demonstrate prolonged hyperfocus on intrinsically motivating tasks. The core deficit is in regulating attention — sustaining it on low-interest tasks and disengaging from high-interest ones when required. ADHD affects approximately 5–7% of children and 2–5% of adults worldwide and persists in some form into adulthood in the majority of cases.

If you consistently score well below age norms on sustained attention tasks and notice significant functional impact in daily life, a formal assessment by a psychologist or psychiatrist is worthwhile.

Research Finding: Mindfulness-based attention training (MBAT) has shown statistically significant improvements in sustained attention and vigilance in multiple randomised controlled trials, with effect sizes comparable to those seen with stimulant medication in non-ADHD adults.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • What is a normal attention span?

    There is no single number. Sustained attention span is highly task-dependent — motivation, novelty, and environmental demands all modulate how long anyone can maintain focus. Under optimal conditions, healthy adults aged 18–50 can sustain focused attention for 35–50 minutes before performance begins to noticeably degrade. The widely-cited "8-second attention span" was a misrepresentation of a 2015 Microsoft study on digital media switching frequency, not sustained attentional capacity.

  • Does attention span decline with age?

    Sustained attention shows a modest decline in older adulthood, particularly from age 65 onward. Adults over 65 typically sustain peak performance for 25–35 minutes compared to 35–50 minutes for younger adults. However, older adults often show improved selective attention (filtering irrelevant information) when motivationally engaged, and experienced adults can compensate for reduced raw capacity through strategy and prior knowledge.

  • How can I improve my attention span?

    Evidence-backed approaches include: sustained attention training tasks (like vigilance and continuous performance tests), mindfulness meditation (which directly targets attentional regulation), aerobic exercise (which improves prefrontal function), reducing environmental interruptions, and improving sleep quality. Regular practice on structured attention tasks provides the most direct route to measurable improvement on standardised assessments.
