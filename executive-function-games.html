About Executive Function Training

Executive functions (EFs) are a set of top-down mental processes that regulate thought and action. The term "executive" reflects the analogy to a corporate executive — someone who does not do the moment-to-moment work themselves, but who coordinates, plans, prioritises, and overrides what the workers (perceptual and motor systems) would automatically do. Neuroanatomically, EFs are predominantly supported by the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and its connections with the basal ganglia, anterior cingulate cortex, and parietal regions.

The most influential scientific framework for understanding executive function was provided by Akira Miyake and colleagues in their landmark 2000 paper in Cognitive Psychology. Using confirmatory factor analysis across a large battery of EF tasks, they identified three core executive functions that are related but distinct:

  • Inhibition — the deliberate suppression of prepotent (dominant, automatic) responses. This is what you use when you stop yourself from saying something impulsive, or when you avoid a habitual action that would be wrong in context. The Stroop color-word task, Go/No-Go, and Stop Signal Task are the canonical measures of inhibitory control. Inhibition failures underlie impulsive behaviour in ADHD, addiction, and borderline personality disorder.
  • Shifting (cognitive flexibility) — the ability to switch attention and cognitive set between different tasks, mental operations, or mental sets. Measured by task-switching paradigms and the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST). Deficits in shifting are characteristic of obsessive-compulsive disorder, autism spectrum conditions, and frontal lobe damage. The "switch cost" — the additional response time incurred on the first trial after a task switch — is a reliable measure of shifting efficiency.
  • Updating (working memory monitoring) — the ongoing monitoring and refreshing of working memory contents as new information makes old information less relevant. Memory updating tasks require replacing old representations with new ones, and are closely related to working memory capacity. Deficits in updating are associated with depression, schizophrenia, and normal aging.

These three functions, while separable, share common neural substrates and show meaningful inter-correlations — which is why EF training often shows positive transfer across paradigms targeting different components. More complex EF behaviours — such as planning, reasoning, and problem solving — are built from combinations of these three core functions.

The developmental trajectory of executive function mirrors the protracted maturation of the prefrontal cortex: EF capacities show dramatic improvement from early childhood through adolescence, continue improving into the mid-20s, peak in early adulthood, and show gradual decline from midlife onwards. This developmental profile has enormous implications for education, parenting, workplace performance, and healthy aging.

How to Improve Your Executive Function

  • Target all three core functions. Because inhibition, shifting, and updating are partially independent, a comprehensive EF training program should include tasks from each domain. Relying only on Stroop-type inhibition tasks will leave shifting and updating largely untrained.
  • Increase challenge progressively. EF improvements are driven by working at the edge of current capacity. Easy tasks produce task-specific automaticity but not EF growth. The games on BrainForge adapt difficulty upward as your performance improves, ensuring you are always in the productive training zone.
  • Practise mindfulness. A growing body of research shows that mindfulness meditation strengthens inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory — converging on all three core EFs. Even brief mindfulness interventions (8 weeks of MBSR) produce measurable prefrontal changes on neuroimaging.
  • Exercise regularly. Aerobic exercise is the most robustly supported lifestyle intervention for EF, with particularly strong effects on inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility. The mechanism involves exercise-induced increases in dopamine and norepinephrine — the primary neurotransmitters of PFC function.
  • Reduce ego-depletion risks. EF capacity fluctuates with glucose availability and mental fatigue. Complex decisions made late in the day or after prolonged effortful tasks show systematically worse EF performance. Training in fresh cognitive states and building sustainable routines that protect cognitive resources will maximise training gains and real-world EF performance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is executive function the same as IQ?

Executive function and IQ are related but distinct constructs. IQ assessments typically include measures of fluid reasoning (Gf), crystallised knowledge (Gc), working memory, and processing speed — and the EF components of inhibition, shifting, and updating correlate with these measures, particularly with fluid reasoning. However, EF is not the same as general intelligence. It is possible to have a high IQ but poor impulse control, or average intelligence but excellent cognitive flexibility. EF measures often predict real-world outcomes such as academic achievement, health behaviours, and financial decision-making above and beyond IQ, because EF captures how well a person actually uses their cognitive capacity under realistic conditions.

Can executive function training help with ADHD?

ADHD is fundamentally a disorder of executive function — particularly inhibitory control and working memory updating. Multiple meta-analyses have confirmed that EF training (including inhibition tasks, working memory training, and cognitive flexibility tasks) produces significant near-transfer effects in individuals with ADHD, improving performance on trained tasks and closely related measures. Far-transfer to classroom behaviour and academic achievement is less consistent. Current guidelines recommend EF training as an adjunct to medication and behavioural therapies rather than a standalone treatment. The games here are appropriate for supplementary cognitive practice, but clinical decisions should always involve a qualified professional.

What is the Stroop effect and why is it so powerful?

The Stroop effect, first described by John Ridley Stroop in 1935, is the observation that people are slower and more error-prone when naming the ink color of a word when the word itself names a different color (e.g., the word "RED" printed in blue ink) compared to when the color and word match (or when naming the color of a color patch). The effect occurs because reading is so highly automated that it produces a prepotent response tendency (saying "red") that must be actively inhibited to produce the correct color-naming response ("blue"). The size of the Stroop interference effect is a direct measure of the conflict between automatic and controlled processing — making it one of the most revealing windows into inhibitory control and the functioning of the anterior cingulate cortex.
