What Does Your Attention Score Mean?

Attention test scores have multiple components — and the combination of hits, omissions, and false alarms tells a more nuanced story than any single number. This guide explains what each metric measures and what your profile reveals about your attentional system.

Types of Attention Scores Explained

Attention is not a single cognitive ability — it encompasses multiple distinct systems. BrainForge tests measure three major types, each with its own scoring metrics.

Continuous Performance Test (CPT) — Sustained Attention

The CPT requires you to respond to target stimuli and withhold responses to non-targets over an extended period (typically 5–10 minutes). It measures your ability to maintain alertness over time. Two numbers define your CPT profile:

  • Hit rate: Percentage of targets you correctly responded to (detecting real signals)
  • False alarm rate: Percentage of non-targets you incorrectly responded to (responding to noise)
CPT Score Classification — Sustained Attention Profile
Hit RateFalse Alarm RateCategoryInterpretation
>90% <5% Excellent Excellent sustained attention; strong vigilance and impulse control
80–90% 5–10% Good Good attention with minor lapses; normal for most adults
70–80% 10–15% Average Some difficulty sustaining attention; may reflect fatigue or distraction
<70% >15% Below Average Significant attention difficulty; possible inattention, impulsivity, or both

SART and d-Prime — Signal Detection

The Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART) and similar paradigms are often analyzed using d-prime (d') — a signal detection theory measure that captures how well you discriminate between targets and non-targets, independent of your response bias.

A d' of 0 means you are performing at chance — no better than random guessing. A d' of 1.5 is typical for healthy adults on SART-style tasks. A d' of 2.5 or above indicates excellent discrimination. The advantage of d' is that it is not inflated by simply responding to everything, making it a purer measure of true attentional sensitivity.

Flanker Task — Selective Attention and Interference

The Flanker task measures your ability to focus on a central stimulus while ignoring distracting flanking stimuli. Your key metric is the congruency effect: the difference in reaction time between congruent trials (flankers point the same direction) and incongruent trials (flankers point the opposite direction).

A congruency effect under 40ms reflects excellent selective attention and minimal interference. A congruency effect of 40–80ms is typical. Above 100ms suggests high susceptibility to distraction — your attention is being captured by irrelevant information.

Understanding Signal Detection: Hits vs. False Alarms vs. d'

Signal detection theory provides a mathematically rigorous way to separate two independent aspects of attention performance:

  • Sensitivity (d'): How well can you actually distinguish targets from non-targets? This is your pure attentional acuity, independent of your response strategy.
  • Response criterion (β): How trigger-happy are you? A liberal criterion means you respond at the slightest suspicion — boosting hit rate but also false alarms. A conservative criterion means you only respond when very certain — reducing false alarms but also hits.

Two people can have identical hit rates but very different false alarm rates — reflecting different strategies rather than different attentional ability. d' disentangles these by combining both metrics into a single sensitivity score.

What High False Alarm Rates Mean vs. High Omission Rates

The pattern of errors on attention tests is often more informative than accuracy alone:

High omission rate (missing targets — low hit rate)

Missing targets while making few false alarms suggests inattention — a failure to detect or register stimuli that were genuinely present. The brain failed to notice or respond in time. This is associated with mind-wandering, low arousal, fatigue, and the inattentive presentation of ADHD.

High false alarm rate (responding to non-targets)

Responding to non-targets while hitting targets reliably suggests impulsivity — difficulty inhibiting responses once a stimulus appears. The brain responds before fully evaluating whether the stimulus is a target. This is associated with the hyperactive-impulsive presentation of ADHD, high arousal states, and caffeine overdose.

Both high omissions and high false alarms

This combined pattern suggests a generally disorganized attentional system that is neither reliably detecting nor reliably inhibiting. This is the most clinically significant pattern and warrants professional evaluation if consistent across sessions.

Important context on ADHD: Attention test scores can raise questions about ADHD — but online tests are emphatically not diagnostic for ADHD. ADHD diagnosis requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation including history, multiple informants, structured interviews, and ruling out other causes. A below-average attention score in isolation does not diagnose anything.

Factors That Temporarily Affect Attention Scores

Before concluding anything from a single attention test, consider these transient factors that reliably suppress performance:

  • Sleep deprivation — even one bad night can reduce hit rate by 15–20%
  • High stress or anxiety — occupies attentional resources needed for the task
  • Distracting environment — external noise or visual distractions split attention
  • Boredom with long tasks — attention naturally wanders in extended vigilance tasks
  • Testing immediately after mental exhaustion — cognitive fatigue reduces impulse control
Key Takeaway: A hit rate above 80% with under 10% false alarms reflects good, healthy sustained attention. The combination of hit rate and false alarm rate matters more than either alone. High omissions suggest inattention; high false alarms suggest impulsivity. Use d-prime as the most reliable single summary of attention performance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good CPT score?

A good CPT score involves a hit rate above 80% combined with a false alarm rate below 10%. Excellent performance is above 90% hits with fewer than 5% false alarms. These thresholds vary slightly by test design and stimulus rate — faster stimulus presentations typically yield higher false alarm rates. Always consider both numbers together rather than just one.

What does a high false alarm rate mean?

High false alarms indicate difficulty suppressing responses — you're responding before fully evaluating whether a stimulus is a target. This reflects impulsive responding rather than inattention per se. Temporarily high false alarms are common when you're over-caffeinated, highly aroused, or rushing. If consistently high across sessions, it suggests reduced inhibitory control, which can be trained with regular Go/No-Go and impulse control tasks.

Is attention trainable?

Yes. Sustained attention improves significantly with regular practice on vigilance tasks, particularly when tasks are sufficiently challenging to require effort. The Flanker congruency effect — a measure of selective attention — reduces with training. Aerobic exercise has the strongest evidence for improving attentional control across all attention subtypes. Mindfulness meditation has moderate evidence for reducing mind-wandering and improving sustained attention over 8-week programs.
