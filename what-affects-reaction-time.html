Reaction Time

What Affects Reaction Time?

Nine factors — from sleep and caffeine to age and stimulus type — can shift your reaction time by anywhere from 10 to 100 milliseconds. Here is what the research shows.

Why Reaction Time Varies

Reaction time (RT) looks simple — press a button when you see a light — but the milliseconds it takes are the product of an intricate chain of neural events: sensory transduction, signal transmission, perceptual identification, response selection, and motor execution. Each link in that chain is influenced by biological and environmental factors. Understanding which factors matter most — and by how much — lets you make informed decisions about when to test, what to avoid, and how to improve.

The 9 Key Factors

1. Age

Reaction time is fastest between ages 22 and 25, after which it declines at roughly 1 ms per year through middle age, then accelerates to 3–5 ms per year after 60. This reflects the gradual loss of myelination, reduction in dopaminergic signalling, and slower prefrontal processing. By age 70, average simple RT is approximately 30–40% slower than at age 25. However, older adults with high aerobic fitness show substantially smaller declines, suggesting the trajectory is not entirely fixed.

2. Sleep Deprivation

Sleep is the single most powerful modifiable factor for day-to-day RT. Harrison and Horne (2000) found that 24 hours of sustained wakefulness slowed RT by approximately 24% — comparable to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.10%. More practically, chronic mild restriction (6 hours per night for two weeks) produces RT deficits equivalent to two full nights of total sleep deprivation. The alarming aspect is that sleep-deprived individuals consistently underestimate their own impairment.

3. Caffeine

Caffeine is a genuine RT enhancer. In a review of the literature, Lorist and Tops (2003) found that doses of 150–300 mg reduce simple RT by 10–20 ms and choice RT by 20–30 ms. The mechanism is adenosine receptor blockade, which reduces tonic neural inhibition and elevates arousal. Benefits are most pronounced in non-habituated users and in conditions of mild fatigue. Doses above 400–500 mg produce diminishing returns and may cause anxiety-related interference.

4. Alcohol

Even modest alcohol consumption degrades RT significantly. At a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05% (below most legal driving limits), RT slows by 20–30 ms. At 0.08%, the impairment approaches 50 ms or more. Alcohol impairs both sensory processing speed and motor response initiation, and crucially, it increases RT variability — the dangerous unpredictability of response timing. Williamson and Feyer (2000) demonstrated that moderate sleep deprivation impairs driving-relevant RT more than legal alcohol limits, a finding that reshaped thinking about drowsy driving.

5. Stimulus Modality

Not all stimuli are processed equally fast. The order from fastest to slowest is: auditory (mean RT ~160 ms), then tactile/somatosensory (~150–180 ms for direct contact stimuli, longer for distal vibration), then visual (~250 ms). Auditory RT is faster because the cochlea converts acoustic energy to neural signals with less latency than the retina converts light, and auditory pathways to motor areas are shorter. This is why sprinters react to a starter's gun rather than a flash of light.

6. Foreperiod and Expectation

The interval between a warning signal ("ready…") and the imperative stimulus strongly affects RT. A foreperiod of 1–2 seconds typically produces the fastest RTs because it matches the optimal motor preparation window. Very short foreperiods (<300 ms) do not allow enough preparation; very long ones (>4 seconds) lead to decay of the prepared state. Predictable foreperiods produce faster RT than variable ones, confirming that anticipation is a real driver of speed — and also explaining why false starts are a genuine risk in sport.

7. Practice and Training

With consistent practice, simple RT can improve by 20–30% over 4–6 weeks. The gains come from multiple sources: faster perceptual identification of the stimulus, more efficient response selection, and reduced trial-to-trial variability. Importantly, practice reduces RT variability (standard deviation) as much as or more than it reduces mean RT — and variability is arguably a better measure of neural efficiency than the mean alone. 8. Task Complexity: Hick's Law

When the number of possible stimuli and responses increases, RT increases in a predictable logarithmic fashion. This is Hick's Law (1952): each doubling of the number of stimulus-response choices adds approximately 150 ms to RT. A simple "press when you see green" task takes ~250 ms; a "press left for red, right for green, space for blue" task may take ~550 ms. This is why experienced practitioners in high-complexity domains — surgeons, air traffic controllers, pilots — train specific stimulus-response mappings until they become automatic, effectively converting choice RT back toward simple RT.

9. Physical Fitness

Aerobic fitness is one of the most consistently replicated biological predictors of RT. Individuals with higher VO2 max show faster RT and lower RT variability. The mechanisms include better cardiovascular delivery of oxygen and glucose to neural tissue, elevated BDNF promoting myelination and synaptic health, and lower baseline inflammation. A meta-analysis by Etnier et al. (2006) confirmed significant effects of aerobic fitness on RT and processing speed across age groups.

Research Highlight: "Moderate sleep deprivation produces impairments in cognitive and motor performance equivalent to legally prescribed levels of alcohol intoxication" (Williamson & Feyer, 2000). Most people driving on six hours of sleep are as impaired as someone at the legal alcohol limit — but feel fine.

Why RT Varies Trial to Trial

Even under identical conditions, RT fluctuates across trials. This intra-individual variability (IIV) is not mere measurement noise — it reflects genuine moment-to-moment fluctuations in attentional state, neural readiness, and arousal. IIV is actually a stronger predictor of cognitive health than mean RT: higher variability is associated with poorer general cognitive ability, greater age-related decline, and early signs of neurological conditions. A good RT measurement strategy therefore always includes a measure of variability (typically the standard deviation across trials) alongside the mean or median.

The Preparatory Interval Effect

Before a reaction time task trial begins, the brain enters a state of motor readiness called the contingent negative variation (CNV), detectable with EEG. The size of the CNV predicts how fast the subsequent RT will be: larger, sustained readiness correlates with faster responses. This preparatory state is trainable. Athletes in sprint and racket sports spend years learning to optimise their preparation window to match the temporal demands of their sport, producing faster and more consistent responses than non-athletes facing the same stimuli.

Key Takeaway: The largest controllable factors on any given testing day are sleep (±50 ms), caffeine (±15 ms), and acute stress. Get eight hours of sleep, moderate caffeine, and test in the afternoon for your best score.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does caffeine really improve reaction time?

Yes. Doses of 150–300 mg of caffeine (roughly 1–3 cups of coffee) consistently reduce simple and choice reaction time by 10–20 ms in controlled studies. The mechanism is adenosine receptor blockade, which increases alertness and reduces neural inhibition. The effect peaks around 30–60 minutes after ingestion. Very high doses (>500 mg) can impair RT through increased anxiety and tremor.

Why is my reaction time worse in the morning?

Reaction time follows a circadian rhythm, typically being slowest in the early morning (6–9 am) and fastest in the late afternoon or early evening (4–8 pm). This reflects the circadian variation in core body temperature, arousal neurotransmitter levels (norepinephrine, dopamine), and prefrontal cortex excitability. For best RT test scores, test yourself in the afternoon after a light meal and moderate activity.

Does practice help reaction time?

Yes. Repeated practice on reaction time tasks produces gains of 20–30% over 4–6 weeks. These improvements come from both perceptual learning (faster stimulus recognition) and motor preparation (readier response systems). Gains are largest in the early sessions and then plateau. Some transfer to untrained tasks occurs, particularly to tasks sharing the same modality and response type.
