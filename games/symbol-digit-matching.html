How to Play

A key table at the top of the game maps a set of symbols to digits. A single symbol is displayed large in the centre of the screen. Your task is to find that symbol in the key table and click (or type) its matching digit as quickly as possible. The next symbol appears immediately after your response — the goal is to process as many correct matches as you can before time runs out.

  • Early levels (1–3): only 4 symbols in the key — use click buttons.
  • Mid levels (7–15): full 9-symbol key, increasing time pressure.
  • Advanced levels (16+): keyboard input required for speed.
  • Expert levels (22–30): 14 symbols in the key; some levels rotate the key every trial.

Score = percentage of correct responses out of all attempted. Pass threshold is 80%.

What It Measures

The Symbol Digit Matching Test directly measures processing speed — how quickly the brain can perform a simple but effortful cognitive operation repeatedly. Unlike reaction time tasks, it requires looking up an association in working memory and executing the correct motor response, making it sensitive to:

  • Psychomotor processing speed
  • Visual scanning efficiency
  • Working memory and associative learning
  • Sustained attention under time pressure
  • Cognitive flexibility (key-rotation levels)

Research

The Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) was developed by Aaron Smith in 1982 as a brief, sensitive measure of brain dysfunction. Participants are given 90 seconds to pair symbols with their corresponding digits using a reference key — a format almost identical to what BrainForge implements here.

The SDMT is one of the most widely used neuropsychological screening tools in clinical practice. It is part of the Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS) battery and has demonstrated sensitivity to subtle cognitive slowing in MS, traumatic brain injury, HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, schizophrenia, and healthy ageing.

Because the task can be administered orally (spoken responses) or in written form, and now digitally with button presses, it remains versatile across populations. Processing speed measured by SDMT predicts functional outcomes including employment status, driving ability, and medication management in clinical samples (Sheridan et al., 2006; Benedict et al., 2006).

FAQ

Why does the key table stay visible throughout the game?

This is faithful to the original SDMT design — the key is always present because the task is measuring how quickly you can look up and execute associations, not whether you have memorised them. Over the course of a trial, most people develop increasing familiarity with the key through incidental learning, which itself reflects encoding speed and working memory efficiency. Advanced levels with a rotating key remove this advantage.

Is typing faster than clicking?

For most people, yes — especially at higher digit counts. Keyboard input removes the motor time needed to visually locate and click a button, allowing pure cognitive throughput to dominate the score. This is why BrainForge introduces keyboard-only mode at Level 16. If you find the keyboard levels noticeably harder, it may reflect a bottleneck in translating visual lookup into motor execution rather than in the lookup itself.

What is a typical SDMT score?

In standard clinical administration (90-second written or oral test), healthy adults aged 20–49 typically produce 55–70 correct responses. Performance declines with age; adults over 70 average around 35–45. On BrainForge's digital version the stimulus changes immediately after response, meaning the pacing differs from paper administration — your response count should not be compared directly to published norms, but relative improvement across sessions is meaningful.
