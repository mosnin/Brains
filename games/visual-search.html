How to Play

A grid of symbols will appear on screen. Your goal is to find and click the target symbol shown at the top of the grid. The target appears exactly once; every other cell is a distractor. Click the correct symbol as quickly as possible to score maximum points. If you click the wrong cell, the trial is marked incorrect and the correct target is highlighted before the next trial begins.

As levels progress the grid grows larger, distractors become more similar to the target (conjunction search), and time limits per trial are introduced. You need at least 80% accuracy across 10 trials to pass each level.

What It Measures

The Visual Search Task directly measures selective attention — the ability to focus on task-relevant information while filtering out irrelevant distractors. Early levels test feature search, where the target pops out immediately due to a unique property (colour or shape). Later levels introduce conjunction search, requiring serial inspection of items and engaging top-down attentional control. Performance metrics include accuracy, response time, and resistance to distractor interference.

Research Background

The visual search paradigm was formalised by Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade (1980) in their influential Feature Integration Theory (FIT). FIT proposes that basic features (colour, orientation, shape) are processed in parallel across the visual field, while conjunctions of features require focused serial attention. The theory introduced the concept of a master map of locations and feature maps that feed into object recognition. Subsequent work by Jeremy Wolfe (Guided Search model, 1994) showed that bottom-up salience and top-down knowledge guide the deployment of attention during search, dramatically reducing search times compared to purely random scanning. Modern neuroimaging studies implicate the fronto-parietal attention network — including the intraparietal sulcus and frontal eye fields — in controlling search priority maps.

FAQ

What is the difference between feature search and conjunction search?

In feature search the target differs from all distractors in a single basic attribute (e.g., a red item among blue items). It pops out automatically and search time is nearly constant regardless of how many distractors are present. In conjunction search the target is defined by a combination of features (e.g., a red vertical bar among red horizontal bars and blue vertical bars). This requires serial, item-by-item inspection and search time increases with set size.

How does this game improve my attention?

Repeated practice with visual search tasks strengthens the neural circuits responsible for deploying top-down attentional control. Training has been shown to speed target detection, reduce distractor interference, and improve the efficiency of attentional guidance — skills that transfer to everyday tasks such as reading, driving, and locating objects in cluttered environments.

Why do later levels feel so much harder?

At higher levels the distractors are deliberately chosen to share visual features with the target — a technique called feature overlap. This eliminates the pop-out effect, forcing your visual system to engage attentional resources for each item inspected. Combined with stricter time limits, this creates a demanding test of both accuracy and processing speed under interference.
