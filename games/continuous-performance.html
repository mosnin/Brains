How to Play the Continuous Performance Test

The Continuous Performance Test (CPT) presents a rapid sequence of letters on screen, one at a time. Your task is simple but demanding: press SPACE (or the on-screen button) only when you see the letter X. For every other letter, stay still. The test measures how consistently you can maintain this rule over many trials — even as your mind drifts or speed increases.

  • Hit: You pressed when X appeared — correct response.
  • Miss: X appeared but you didn't press — attention lapse.
  • False Alarm: You pressed for a non-X letter — impulsive response.

Your score is calculated as (Hits + Correct Rejections) / Total Stimuli × 100. Pass each level by scoring 80% or above. Thirty progressive levels ramp up speed, reduce target frequency, and introduce confusable letters like K to challenge your focus.

What the Continuous Performance Test Measures

The CPT assesses two core cognitive processes that underpin daily functioning:

  • Sustained attention: The ability to maintain alert, consistent focus over an extended period without deterioration in performance.
  • Inhibitory control: The ability to suppress automatic responses to non-target stimuli — resisting the urge to press when you shouldn't.

Miss rates reflect attention failures, while false alarm rates reflect impulsivity. Together they offer a nuanced picture of attentional control that simple reaction-time tasks cannot capture.

Research Behind the CPT

The Continuous Performance Test was pioneered by Rosvold et al. (1956) as a measure of sustained attention in patients with brain lesions. Decades later, Keith Conners developed the Conners' CPT-II, a standardised clinical instrument widely used to evaluate ADHD and attention deficits. Research consistently shows that CPT performance — particularly signal detection metrics derived from hits and false alarms — discriminates between clinical and non-clinical populations and tracks treatment response. Regular training on CPT-style tasks has been linked to improvements in vigilance and reduced mind-wandering in healthy adults.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this the same as the clinical Conners CPT-II?

This game is inspired by and follows the core paradigm of the Conners CPT-II but is not a clinical diagnostic tool. It is designed for cognitive training and self-assessment. For clinical evaluation, please consult a licensed neuropsychologist.

How long does each level take?

Level duration scales with difficulty. Early levels with 40 stimuli at 1500 ms ISI take about one minute; later levels with 100 stimuli at 450 ms ISI take around 45 seconds. Most levels fall between 30 seconds and 90 seconds.

Why does the test get harder with fewer targets?

When X appears less frequently (down to 10% in later levels), your brain cannot rely on frequent confirmation that you're in the right state of readiness. Fewer targets demand a higher and more consistent state of vigilance across longer stretches of non-target stimuli.

What does a high false alarm rate mean?

A high false alarm rate suggests impulsivity — your response system is too "trigger-happy." This is distinct from an attention problem and maps to poor inhibitory control. Both can be trained independently through practice.

Can this test detect ADHD?

No. While CPT measures correlate with ADHD symptoms in research settings, a single online game cannot diagnose any condition. ADHD is diagnosed by qualified clinicians using multiple assessment tools, clinical history, and standardised norms. Use this game for training and self-awareness only.
