How to Play

Type A (Levels 1–12): Circles numbered 1 through N are scattered on the canvas. Click or tap them in ascending order: 1 → 2 → 3 → and so on. A line draws between each correctly connected node.

Type B (Levels 13–30): The sequence alternates between numbers and letters: 1 → A → 2 → B → 3 → C… This requires mental set-shifting between two categories simultaneously.

The current target node pulses with a teal glow to guide you. Wrong clicks flash red but do not end the trial — correct yourself and continue. Your score is based on correctly connected nodes, with a time limit that tightens as levels advance.

What It Measures

The Trail Making Test is one of the most widely used neuropsychological assessments. It evaluates three core cognitive domains:

  • Visual scanning and sequencing (Type A) — the ability to quickly scan a visual field and execute a planned sequence.
  • Cognitive flexibility / set-shifting (Type B) — the ability to alternate mental sets, dividing attention between two interleaved sequences.
  • Processing speed — how efficiently you execute the task under time pressure.

The ratio of Type B completion time to Type A completion time (B/A ratio) is a classic index of executive dysfunction: a high ratio suggests disproportionate difficulty with the set-shifting demand beyond basic sequencing speed.

Research Background

The Trail Making Test was originally developed by Partington & Leiter (1949) as the “Partington Pathways Test” and later adapted and standardized by Ralph Reitan (1958) as part of the Halstead–Reitan Neuropsychological Test Battery. It remains a standard tool in clinical neuropsychology for detecting brain injury, dementia, and frontal lobe dysfunction.

Normative data from large samples (Tombaugh, 2004; Strauss, Sherman & Spreen, 2006) show strong age and education effects, with completion time increasing meaningfully after age 60. The test discriminates reliably between healthy adults and patients with Alzheimer’s disease, traumatic brain injury, and schizophrenia.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between TMT-A and TMT-B?

TMT-A requires connecting numbered circles in ascending order (1–2–3…). It primarily measures visual-motor speed and attention. TMT-B requires alternating between numbers and letters (1–A–2–B…), adding a set-shifting demand that taxes the prefrontal cortex. Clinicians often subtract A time from B time, or compute the B/A ratio, to isolate the executive function component independent of motor speed.

Can I improve my Trail Making Test score with practice?

Yes — practice effects are well-documented in the TMT literature. Repeated testing yields faster completion times, largely due to learning the task demands rather than broad cognitive improvement. That said, the set-shifting skill engaged by Type B does transfer to real-world tasks requiring mental flexibility, such as multitasking and task-switching in dynamic environments.

How does the online version compare to the clinical paper version?

The clinical version is administered on paper with a pencil; the examiner records total time and error count. This online version captures the same sequencing and set-shifting demands using a touchscreen or mouse. Key differences: the online version randomizes node positions each attempt, includes a live timer displayed to the player (which may increase pressure), and uses click/tap rather than pencil drawing. Normative comparisons to paper-and-pencil standards should therefore be made cautiously.
