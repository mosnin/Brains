How to Play the Stop Signal Task

Each trial begins with a fixation cross, followed by an arrow pointing left (←) or right (→). Your primary task — the Go task — is to classify the arrow direction as quickly as possible:

  • Press the Left arrow key (or tap the Left button on mobile) for a leftward arrow ←.
  • Press the Right arrow key (or tap the Right button on mobile) for a rightward arrow →.
  • Respond as fast as you can — your Go reaction time directly affects your overall score.

On approximately 15–30% of trials (increasing with level), a red STOP signal flashes on screen a short time after the arrow appears. When you see the STOP signal, withhold your response completely — do not press any key or button, even if your finger is already moving toward the response. Successfully stopping on these trials is the core inhibitory challenge of the task.

The Stop-Signal Delay (SSD) — the time between the arrow appearing and the STOP signal — adapts automatically throughout each level. If you successfully inhibit your response, the SSD increases by 50 ms on the next stop trial, making the next stop harder. If you fail to stop, the SSD decreases by 50 ms, making the next stop easier. This staircase tracking procedure converges on the SSD where you stop approximately 50% of the time — the mathematically optimal point for estimating your Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT). Score 75% or higher to advance.

What the Stop Signal Task Measures

The Stop Signal Task is widely regarded as the most methodologically rigorous paradigm for measuring response inhibition — specifically, the ability to cancel a motor response that is already in progress. Unlike the Go/No-Go task where you withhold before initiating a response, the SST requires canceling a response mid-flight.

  • Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT): The latency of your internal inhibitory process, estimated as mean Go RT minus the converged SSD. SSRT in healthy adults averages approximately 200 ms and reflects how quickly your inhibitory system can override an already-initiated motor program.
  • Motor impulse control: The primary clinical application — individuals with ADHD, addiction disorders, and OCD all show characteristic abnormalities in SSRT and stop probability that reflect impaired motor impulse control circuits.
  • Go task accuracy and speed: Correctly identifying arrow direction while maintaining fast responses reveals the baseline Go processing efficiency that the inhibitory race runs against.
  • Inhibition rate on stop trials: The proportion of stop trials successfully inhibited, which the adaptive SSD procedure tracks to maintain near 50% — the point that yields the most statistically reliable SSRT estimate.

Research Background

The Stop Signal Task was formally developed and theoretically grounded by Gordon D. Logan and Matthew C. Cowan (1984), building on earlier reaction time work by Lappin & Eriksen (1966). Their landmark paper introduced the horse-race model of response inhibition: on each stop trial, a Go process and a Stop process race to completion independently and in parallel. Whether the subject stops depends entirely on which process finishes first. The model makes precise, quantitative predictions about inhibition probability as a function of SSD that can be verified empirically — making it one of the most rigorously formalized models in all of cognitive psychology.

A comprehensive review by Verbruggen & Logan (2008) surveyed two decades of SST research across populations, methods, and clinical applications. The review confirmed that the horse-race model holds across a wide range of conditions and that SSRT is a robust individual-difference variable with strong test-retest reliability. SSRT shows meaningful correlations with trait impulsivity, executive function composite scores, and clinical diagnoses — including ADHD (where SSRT is reliably prolonged by 50–100 ms relative to controls), obsessive-compulsive disorder, and substance use disorders.

Neuroimaging research consistently implicates the right inferior frontal cortex (rIFC) and the subthalamic nucleus (STN) — connected via the hyperdirect pathway — as the critical circuitry for implementing stop-signal inhibition. The STN acts as a rapid brake on motor output that can be engaged in time to cancel responses when activated by rIFC signals. Disruption of this circuit, through lesions or pharmacological manipulation, selectively impairs stop-signal performance while leaving Go task performance intact, confirming that inhibition and Go processing rely on partially dissociable neural systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can I not always stop, even when I try hard?

This is not a bug or a reflection of poor effort — it is a fundamental feature of the task by design. The adaptive SSD mechanism is specifically set up to make stopping succeed approximately 50% of the time on stop trials. When the SSD is long (the stop signal appears late), the Go process is too far advanced for the Stop process to win the race, and stopping is genuinely impossible regardless of your intentions or effort. A 50% stop-success rate is actually the mathematically ideal operating point for computing SSRT, because at exactly 50%, the inhibitory process and Go process finish simultaneously on average — giving the cleanest estimate of their relative speeds. So failing about half the time on stop trials is the correct outcome.

What is a good SSRT score?

In healthy adults, SSRT typically falls between 150 ms and 250 ms, with a population mean near 200 ms as reported by Verbruggen & Logan (2008). An SSRT below 200 ms indicates efficient inhibitory processing. Values above 300 ms may suggest weaker inhibitory control, though factors such as age, fatigue, practice effects, and the technical latency of web-based input can all affect the estimate. The SSRT displayed after each level is an approximation using the integration method (mean Go RT minus the converged SSD), which is a good estimate under the assumption that the horse-race model holds and the staircase has converged.

Should I slow down my Go responses to make stopping easier?

No — deliberately slowing your Go responses is the most common mistake new users make, and it fundamentally invalidates the task. The horse-race model only yields a valid SSRT estimate when Go responses are made as quickly as possible. If you slow down your Go process, the inhibitory process wins more easily not because inhibition improved but because the race became unfair. This produces an artificially short (misleadingly favorable) SSRT estimate and defeats the purpose of the measurement. The correct approach is to treat the Go task as a pure speed task and let the adaptive SSD mechanism handle the difficulty of the stop trials automatically.
