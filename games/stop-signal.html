Stop Signal Task

Respond to arrows — but stop immediately when you see ✕. Tests inhibitory control and measures your Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT).

How to Play the Stop Signal Task

Each trial begins with a fixation cross, then an arrow (← or →) appears. Your goal is to press the matching key as fast as possible. On roughly 25% of trials a red ✕ stop signal appears shortly after the arrow. When you see ✕ you must withhold your response — do not press any key.

  • Use the Left arrow key (or Left button) for ← arrows.
  • Use the Right arrow key (or Right button) for → arrows.
  • When ✕ appears — stop immediately, no matter how certain you were about pressing.
  • Score is based on combined Go accuracy and inhibition success rate. Pass 80% to advance.

The Stop-Signal Delay (SSD) adapts each trial. Stop successfully and the delay lengthens, making the next stop harder. Fail to stop and the delay shortens. This staircase procedure homes in on your inhibition threshold automatically.

What It Measures

The Stop Signal Task measures inhibitory control — the ability to cancel a motor response that is already being prepared. The key metric is the Stop-Signal Reaction Time (SSRT), estimated as the mean Go RT minus the converged SSD. SSRT reflects the speed of your internal inhibition process, which runs in parallel with (and races against) your Go process.

Typical SSRT in healthy adults ranges from 150–250 ms. Longer SSRT is associated with ADHD, addiction, and other conditions involving impaired inhibitory control. Shorter SSRT indicates faster and more efficient inhibition. Because this is a covert latency that cannot be directly observed, the race model and staircase tracking method are used to estimate it.

Research Background

The Stop Signal Task was formalised by Gordon D. Logan and Matthew C. Cowan (1984), building on earlier work by Lappin and Eriksen (1966). Their race model posits that the Go process and a countermanding Stop process race to completion; whether you stop depends on which process finishes first. The model generates quantitative predictions about inhibition probability and SSRT that can be verified empirically.

The task has become a cornerstone of cognitive neuroscience. fMRI studies implicate the right inferior frontal cortex and the subthalamic nucleus (via the hyperdirect pathway) in stop-signal inhibition. Deficits in stop-signal inhibition are among the most replicated findings in ADHD research, making computerised versions like this one valuable for both research and public education.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is stopping sometimes impossible even when I try?

That is expected and by design. The adaptive SSD ensures you stop successfully about 50% of the time on stop trials. When SSD is long enough, the stop signal arrives too late for your inhibition process to win the race, and stopping becomes impossible regardless of effort. A failure rate around 50% on stop trials is actually the mathematically ideal point for measuring SSRT, so it is a feature, not a flaw.

What is a good SSRT score?

Typical healthy adult SSRT is 150–250 ms. Under 200 ms is considered fast inhibition. Above 300 ms may suggest weaker inhibitory control, though factors like fatigue, age, and practice all affect the estimate. The value displayed after each level is an approximation based on the staircase-tracking method (GoRT minus converged SSD).

Should I slow down to stop more easily?

You should not deliberately slow your Go responses. The race model only yields a valid SSRT estimate when Go responses are made as fast as possible. Intentionally slowing down biases the SSRT estimate upward and defeats the purpose of the task. The go and stop instructions are intentionally in conflict — embrace it.
