How to Play

Each round begins with a brief flash of a grid pattern — circles arranged in a grid, some filled and some empty. The pattern disappears after a short interval (ranging from 1,500 ms at beginner levels down to just 100 ms at expert levels). Your task is to select which of the four displayed options exactly matches the original pattern.

There are 10 rounds per level. Pass with 80% or more correct to unlock the next level. As you progress, grids grow from 3×3 to 6×6, and the flash duration shortens dramatically — pushing the limits of your perceptual speed.

What It Measures

This task directly targets visual processing speed — how quickly your brain can encode a visual scene into working memory. It also taxes perceptual speed, a core component of fluid intelligence, and short-term visual memory, since you must hold the pattern in mind after it disappears. The distractor choices require you to discriminate fine spatial differences, adding a component of pattern discrimination.

Research Background

Processing speed is one of the most studied cognitive constructs. Jensen (2006) demonstrated strong correlations between elementary perceptual speed measures and general intelligence (g), suggesting that raw speed of information encoding underlies higher-order cognition. The Processing Speed Tests (PST) battery and its predecessors have long used brief visual exposures (tachistoscopes historically, now software) to assess perceptual encoding rate. Research by Salthouse (1996) showed that age-related cognitive decline is largely mediated by processing speed slowing, making this kind of training particularly relevant for healthy aging.

FAQ

Why does the pattern disappear so quickly at higher levels?

The flash duration is the key manipulation. At 100 ms, the pattern is visible for roughly one saccade cycle — your eye barely has time to fixate on the stimulus before it is gone. This forces your visual system to rely on iconic memory, a brief high-capacity but rapidly-decaying store, and to rapidly transfer information to working memory. Training at these durations is thought to improve the speed of that transfer.

How are the wrong choices (distractors) generated?

Each distractor is created by swapping one or two filled cells with empty cells in the correct pattern. At easier levels, distractors differ by two cells, making them easier to reject. At harder levels, distractors differ by only one cell, requiring very precise encoding to identify the correct pattern.

Is this test a valid measure of my IQ or cognitive ability?

This game is designed for training and entertainment, not clinical assessment. While the underlying construct — perceptual speed — correlates moderately with IQ measures in research settings, a single casual game cannot produce a valid or reliable psychometric estimate of intelligence. For clinical cognitive assessment, please consult a qualified neuropsychologist.
