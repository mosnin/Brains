Stroop Color-Word Test

How to Play the Stroop Test

Each trial shows a color word (e.g., the word "RED") printed in an ink color that may or may not match the word itself. Your task is to click the button matching the ink color — completely ignoring what the word says. Early levels begin with congruent trials (word and ink match) to build familiarity. As you progress, more incongruent trials appear and response time windows shrink, ramping up the cognitive demand across all 30 levels.

  • Click the colored button that matches the ink color of the displayed word.
  • Ignore the word's meaning — focus only on the color it is printed in.
  • Respond before the timer expires. Score 80% or higher to advance.
  • Levels 1–3 are fully congruent to warm up; incongruent trials increase progressively.
  • Levels 28–30 feature 100% incongruent trials with only 1.2 seconds per response.

What the Stroop Test Measures

The Stroop Color-Word Test is one of the most robust measures of executive function in cognitive psychology. It specifically targets:

  • Inhibitory control — suppressing the automatic, dominant response (reading the word) in favor of a controlled response (naming the ink color).
  • Selective attention — attending to one stimulus dimension (ink color) while filtering out another (word meaning).
  • Cognitive interference — the "Stroop effect" is the processing cost that arises when two stimulus features conflict.
  • Processing speed under conflict — the ability to resolve interference quickly under time pressure.

Research Background

First described by John Ridley Stroop in his landmark 1935 paper "Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions", the Stroop task has been replicated thousands of times and remains a cornerstone of neuropsychological assessment. The Stroop interference effect — slower and more error-prone responses to incongruent stimuli — is one of the most reliable findings in cognitive psychology.

Neuroimaging studies (e.g., MacLeod, 1991; Milham et al., 2001) consistently implicate the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and prefrontal cortex in resolving Stroop conflict. Deficits in Stroop performance are associated with ADHD, traumatic brain injury, aging, and conditions affecting frontal lobe function. Regular practice on interference tasks like the Stroop test is associated with improvements in broader inhibitory control (Diamond, 2013).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it harder to name the ink color when the word says a different color?

Reading is a highly practiced, automatic process — your brain processes the word's meaning faster than it processes ink color. When the two conflict, your executive control system must actively suppress the faster "read the word" response and select the slower "name the color" response instead. This competition creates the Stroop interference effect: slower reaction times and more errors on incongruent trials.

What score is considered "good" on the Stroop test?

In this implementation, passing requires 80% accuracy per level. In clinical settings, performance is typically measured as reaction time rather than accuracy alone. Healthy adults typically show robust interference effects (200–300ms slower on incongruent vs. congruent trials) but high accuracy. Younger adults generally outperform older adults on response speed, while accuracy differences are smaller across age groups.

Can practicing the Stroop test improve my inhibitory control?

Research supports the idea that repeated practice on Stroop-like inhibitory control tasks produces task-specific improvements, and some studies show near-transfer to related executive function tasks. A 2013 meta-analysis by Diamond and Ling found moderate evidence for cognitive training benefits. However, improvements are strongest for the trained task itself and may be smaller for untrained daily-life situations. Combining multiple cognitive training paradigms (like those on BrainForge) is the recommended approach for broader benefits.
