Spatial N-Back Test

Track grid positions across N trials back to strengthen visual-spatial working memory.

Watch the highlighted cell each trial. Press Position Match (or Space / A) when the current position matches the one from N trials ago.

How to Play Spatial N-Back

Each round consists of a series of trials. In every trial, one cell in a 4×4 (or 5×5 at higher levels) grid lights up briefly. Your goal is to decide whether the cell that just lit up is in the same position as the cell that appeared exactly N trials earlier.

Press the Position Match button — or tap Space / A on your keyboard — whenever you detect a match. Stay silent (do nothing) when the position is new. You can respond during the stimulus or the blank gap that follows, so you always have a generous window.

Levels 1–18 use a 4×4 grid with N ranging from 1 to 3. From Level 19 onwards the grid expands to 5×5 (25 cells) and N climbs to 7, dramatically increasing the spatial load. Reach 80% accuracy to unlock the next level.

What It Measures

The Spatial N-Back is a widely used measure of visual-spatial working memory — the ability to temporarily store and mentally manipulate information about where objects appear in space. This faculty is central to navigation, mental rotation, engineering design, and many everyday tasks.

As N increases the task demands a larger mental buffer: you must hold and continuously update a list of recent positions, dropping the oldest and adding the newest with each trial. This places heavy load on the visuospatial sketchpad component of working memory described in Baddeley's model.

Your score is calculated as: (Hits + Correct Rejections) / Total Trials × 100. A hit is correctly detecting a match; a correct rejection is correctly ignoring a non-match. False alarms (pressing when there is no match) and misses (not pressing when there is a match) both reduce accuracy.

Research Background

The N-Back paradigm was introduced by Wayne Kirchner in 1958 as a clinical tool to assess working memory and prefrontal lobe function. It was substantially popularised in neuroscience research through the landmark 2008 study by Jaeggi, Buschkuehl, Jonides, and Perrig, who found that adaptive N-Back training produced gains in fluid intelligence — the ability to reason and solve novel problems — that transferred beyond the trained task.

Neuroimaging studies consistently show that N-Back performance activates the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the parietal cortex, regions associated with executive control and spatial processing. Higher N values recruit additional frontal resources and are associated with greater DLPFC activation.

While debates continue about the size and specificity of transfer effects, a broad consensus supports N-Back training as a reliable, scientifically grounded way to challenge and practise working memory processes in a controlled, measurable manner.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Spatial N-Back test?

The Spatial N-Back test is a working memory task where you watch a series of highlighted positions on a grid and press a button whenever the current position matches the one that appeared N trials earlier. The "N" increases with difficulty, requiring you to hold more positions in working memory simultaneously.

Does N-Back training actually improve working memory?

Research by Jaeggi et al. (2008) showed that N-Back training can improve fluid intelligence and working memory capacity with consistent practice. While findings vary across studies, regular training at the edge of your ability — where you are neither too comfortable nor overwhelmed — is associated with the greatest cognitive benefits.

How is the Spatial N-Back different from the Dual N-Back?

The Spatial N-Back uses only one stimulus stream: grid positions. The Dual N-Back adds a simultaneous auditory stream (spoken letters), requiring you to track two independent sequences at once. Spatial N-Back is a great starting point for beginners, while Dual N-Back places a significantly higher load on divided attention and working memory.
