Go/No-Go Task

A fundamental test of response inhibition and impulse control. Press SPACE (or tap GO) for the green circle (Go trial) and withhold completely for the red square (No-Go trial). Measures your ability to stop automatic responses before they become actions.

How to Play the Go/No-Go Task

Each trial displays a shape in the center of the screen after a brief fixation cross. Your single job is to decide, in under a second, whether that shape warrants a response:

  • Green circle — GO trial: Press Space or tap the on-screen GO button as quickly as you can. These are the majority of trials, making the pressing response feel automatic and habitual.
  • Red square — NO-GO trial: Do absolutely nothing. Keep your hand still, resist the urge to press, and let the stimulus disappear on its own. Any key press or button tap on a NO-GO trial counts as a commission error.

The 30 levels systematically increase difficulty in two ways. First, the inter-stimulus interval shrinks: early levels give you 1,500 ms between stimuli, while late levels compress this to 550 ms, reducing the time your inhibitory control system has to prepare. Second, the ratio of NO-GO trials rises from 20% (Level 1) to 35% (Level 30), which paradoxically makes inhibition harder — the more often you see a GO, the stronger the prepotent press response becomes. Scoring 80% or higher on any level unlocks the next.

Your score on each level equals: (correct GO presses + correct NO-GO withholdings) ÷ total trials × 100%. Commission errors (pressing on NO-GO) and omission errors (failing to press on GO) both reduce your score.

What the Go/No-Go Task Measures

The Go/No-Go paradigm is the most widely used laboratory measure of response inhibition — the ability to suppress an action that has already been prepared or is about to be executed. This capacity is a cornerstone of executive function and daily impulse control.

  • Response inhibition: The core construct — can you stop a habitual pressing response when context demands it? Commission error rate is the primary index of inhibitory failure.
  • Impulsivity: High commission error rates correlate strongly with trait impulsivity, ADHD symptom severity, and substance use disorders across clinical and non-clinical populations.
  • Sustained attention: Omission errors (missed GO trials) reflect lapses in vigilance across the level rather than true inhibitory failures — they separate inattention from impulsivity.
  • Prepotent response suppression: The high GO percentage deliberately builds a strong automatic tendency to press. Inhibiting that tendency when a NO-GO appears requires active top-down executive control mediated by the right inferior frontal cortex.

Research Background

The theoretical roots of the Go/No-Go task trace to Franciscus Donders (1868), who pioneered cognitive chronometry by measuring the time cost of adding a discrimination or selection requirement to a simple reaction time task. Donders's subtraction logic established that different cognitive operations consume measurable time and can be isolated experimentally — a framework that continues to shape cognitive neuroscience today.

The modern Go/No-Go paradigm specifically targets inhibitory control through the NoGo P3 ERP component: a positive-going brain wave peaking around 300–400 ms over right frontal electrodes, which is substantially larger on successful NO-GO trials than on GO trials. This ERP marker is used in research as an index of the neural resources deployed to suppress a response. Crucially, the amplitude of the NoGo P3 is reduced in individuals with ADHD, explaining their higher commission error rates even when they intend to stop.

Clinically, the Go/No-Go task is a standard component of ADHD neuropsychological assessments. Meta-analyses (Willcutt et al., 2005; Lijffijt et al., 2005) consistently show that children and adults with ADHD-Combined presentation commit significantly more commission errors than matched controls, while omission rates are elevated mainly in ADHD-Inattentive presentations. The task is also sensitive to the inhibition-disrupting effects of sleep deprivation, alcohol, and frontal lobe damage, making it a broad-purpose probe of behavioral control.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are most stimuli GO signals?

Maintaining a high proportion of GO trials — typically 70–80% — is deliberate design, not arbitrary convenience. With mostly GO signals, your brain builds a strong prepotent (automatic, dominant) tendency to press on every stimulus. When a rare NO-GO then appears, you must actively inhibit that well-established habit in real time. If NO-GO stimuli were equally frequent, the task would primarily measure decision-making under uncertainty rather than inhibitory control of a prepotent response. The ratio is the key ingredient that creates the inhibitory demand.

What is the difference between a commission error and an omission error?

A commission error (false alarm) means you pressed on a NO-GO stimulus — the inhibitory brake failed and an impulsive response slipped through. A omission error (miss) means you failed to press on a GO stimulus — you either weren't attending or responded too slowly. These two error types have different cognitive interpretations: commission errors primarily reflect impulsivity and poor inhibitory control, while omission errors primarily reflect inattention or motor slowing. Research often reports them separately for this reason.

Can practicing the Go/No-Go Task improve real-world impulse control?

Research on cognitive training transfer is nuanced. Studies by Houben & Wiers (2011) demonstrated that Go/No-Go training specifically directed at food- or alcohol-related stimuli can reduce consumption behavior in laboratory settings. For general impulse control, regular training shows most consistent improvement on the trained task itself and related inhibitory measures. Combining Go/No-Go practice with other executive function exercises — such as the Stop Signal Task and Stroop Test available on BrainForge — is the approach most likely to produce broader benefits, as it engages multiple facets of the inhibitory control system.
