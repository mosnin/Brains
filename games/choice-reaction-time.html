How to Play Choice Reaction Time Test

A stimulus appears on screen — either a coloured circle or a directional arrow. Your job is to press the matching key (or on-screen button) as quickly as possible. Use the Left arrow key for leftward stimuli and the Right arrow key for rightward stimuli. In higher levels you will also need to respond to Up and Down directions. Your score for each level is the percentage of trials you answered correctly within the time threshold. Score 80% or more to pass.

  • Levels 1–5: Coloured circles (Blue = Left, Red = Right). 900 ms threshold.
  • Levels 6–10: Coloured circles with shorter foreperiods. 750 ms threshold.
  • Levels 11–15: Arrow symbols (← →). 700 ms threshold.
  • Levels 16–20: Arrows with unpredictable timing. 650 ms threshold.
  • Levels 21–25: Four-choice arrows (↑ ↓ ← →). 600 ms threshold.
  • Levels 26–30: Four choices with flanker distractors. 550 ms threshold.

What It Measures

Choice reaction time (CRT) is the time between a stimulus and a correct response when multiple stimulus-response pairs are possible. Unlike simple reaction time, CRT requires decision-making, making it sensitive to cognitive processing speed, perceptual discrimination, and response selection. The task is a computerised version of the Donders Type C reaction time paradigm (1868), updated with adaptive difficulty across 30 levels.

Average adult CRT to visual stimuli is approximately 350–500 ms, compared to 180–250 ms for simple reaction time. The extra time reflects the cognitive cost of selecting among alternatives — a phenomenon formalised as Hick's Law (1952), which states that reaction time increases logarithmically with the number of equally probable choices.

Research Background

W.E. Hick (1952) and R. Hyman (1953) independently demonstrated that CRT grows as a linear function of the information content (in bits) of the stimulus set. The Hick-Hyman Law has since been applied in interface design, sports science, and clinical neuropsychology. Slowed CRT is an early marker of cognitive decline, making it a useful screening measure. The four-choice condition in the upper levels of this test adds approximately 50–80 ms of decision time versus the two-choice condition for most adults.

The flanker-distractor condition in levels 26–30 draws on Eriksen & Eriksen (1974), who showed that irrelevant flanking stimuli compatible or incompatible with the target produce measurable interference, isolating the cost of selective attention and response conflict resolution.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good choice reaction time score?

For two-choice tasks most healthy adults respond in 300–450 ms. Under 350 ms is considered fast. For the four-choice condition, add roughly 50–80 ms. Performance varies with age, fatigue, and practice. The thresholds in this test (550–900 ms) are set conservatively so beginners can still score well while leaving room for improvement.

How is this different from a simple reaction time test?

A simple reaction time test has only one stimulus and one response — press any key when you see the flash. Choice reaction time requires you to identify which stimulus appeared and select the matching response. This extra decision step adds 100–300 ms of processing time and is far more sensitive to changes in cognitive speed.

Does practice improve choice reaction time?

Yes. Research consistently shows that practice reduces CRT, primarily through faster response selection rather than faster perceptual processing. The gains are largest early in training and plateau after several hundred trials. This test tracks your performance across 30 levels so you can observe your own improvement over time.
