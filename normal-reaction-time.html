Reaction Time

What Is a Normal Reaction Time?

A "normal" reaction time for a healthy young adult is 200–250 ms for simple RT. This guide explains normal ranges, what influences them, and how to interpret your results.

Defining "Normal" in Reaction Time Research

When scientists talk about a "normal" reaction time, they mean the range that encompasses the majority of a defined population — typically healthy adults tested under controlled conditions with adequate practice. The concept of normality in psychometrics is inherently statistical: normal simply means near the centre of a distribution, not optimal or ideal.

The distribution of simple reaction times across the adult population is approximately log-normal: most scores cluster between 200 and 350 ms, with a long right tail of slower responses. A small number of individuals consistently score below 180 ms, and these are the individuals researchers typically classify as exceptional.

Because RT varies with age, sex, testing modality, hardware, fatigue, and practice, a single universal normal range is necessarily imprecise. The table below provides the most widely cited classification system for visual simple RT in healthy adults.

Category RT Range What It Means Who Achieves It
Exceptional <180 ms Elite neural speed Athletes, gamers, trained individuals
Excellent 180–220 ms Top 15% Young, trained individuals
Good 220–260 ms Above average Healthy young adults
Normal 260–320 ms Average for adults Most healthy adults
Below Average 320–400 ms Worth monitoring Older adults, fatigued individuals
Poor >400 ms May indicate issue Clinical concern under controlled conditions

Important Context

Online tests and lab tests produce different absolute values due to display latency and input device variation. A result of 280 ms on a well-designed online test is roughly equivalent to 250 ms in a laboratory setting. When comparing your score to norms, verify whether the norms were derived from lab or online testing.

Normal Ranges by Test Type

Simple RT norms cannot be directly applied to other reaction time paradigms. Choice RT, discrimination RT, and stop-signal RT all involve additional cognitive processes — decision-making, response inhibition, selective attention — that systematically lengthen response times compared to the simple case.

For a two-choice reaction time task (responding to one of two possible stimuli), the average young adult scores around 380–420 ms. A result below 320 ms is excellent; above 500 ms is below average. For a four-choice task, add roughly another 100 ms to these thresholds across all categories.

The stop-signal task is different: it estimates the stop-signal reaction time (SSRT), an internal inhibition speed rather than an overt response. Normal SSRT is around 200 ms; values above 250 ms suggest reduced inhibitory control and are a marker used in ADHD assessment.

What "Normal" Means Statistically

In a population of 1,000 healthy young adults, simple RT follows a distribution that looks roughly like this: the fastest 5% score below 180 ms, the next 10% score 180–210 ms, the bulk of the population (roughly 70%) score 210–320 ms, and the slowest 15% score above 320 ms. This is why researchers call 220–260 ms "above average" rather than average — the true median for the full adult population (including all ages) is closer to 280–300 ms.

Conditions That Affect Reaction Time

Sleep Deprivation

Missing even one night of sleep degrades simple RT by 30–50 ms on average, and more critically, dramatically increases response variability. Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT) research by Dinges and colleagues shows that after 17–19 hours of wakefulness, RT impairment is equivalent to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%. After 24 hours without sleep, equivalence reaches 0.10%.

Alcohol Consumption

A blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of 0.05% slows simple RT by approximately 30–40 ms and substantially increases the rate of errors and lapses. At legal driving limits in most jurisdictions (0.08% BAC), RT impairment is approximately 50–80 ms and choice RT is even more affected, as alcohol preferentially disrupts higher cognitive processing.

Medications

Benzodiazepines, antihistamines, certain antidepressants, and opioids all slow reaction time by varying amounts. Stimulant medications prescribed for ADHD (methylphenidate, amphetamines) can improve RT in individuals with attentional impairments but have minimal effect on RT in neurotypical adults under normal conditions.

Neurological Conditions

Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, and early-stage Alzheimer's disease all reliably slow RT. In clinical practice, RT slowing — particularly RT variability — is used as a sensitive early biomarker for several of these conditions. A consistent pattern of very slow RTs in someone under 50 without an obvious explanation warrants neurological evaluation.

Research Highlight

A large 2006 study by Der and Deary (n = 7,416) found that simple RT and its variability both predict 10-year mortality independently of IQ and health confounds. Each standard deviation decrease in RT speed was associated with a 25% increase in all-cause mortality, illustrating RT as a genuine biomarker of biological aging rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 300 ms a good reaction time?

A reaction time of 300 ms falls in the average range for healthy adults. It is not exceptional but is entirely normal, particularly for adults over 30 or those who do not regularly train reaction speed. Simple RT of 200–260 ms is considered good for a young adult; 260–320 ms is solidly average for the broader adult population.

What is the world's fastest reaction time?

The fastest verified simple reaction times in research settings approach 100–120 ms, but responses this fast in unsupervised settings often indicate anticipation rather than true reaction. Among elite athletes, times of 140–160 ms for a legitimate visual stimulus are documented. The human physiological minimum for a true visual reaction is approximately 100–120 ms given nerve conduction and motor output delays.

Is my reaction time normal?

For healthy adults aged 18–35, a simple visual reaction time of 200–260 ms is normal. Ages 35–55 typically range 250–310 ms. Above 400 ms on a well-designed test under good conditions may be worth noting and re-testing. Factors like fatigue, medication, and being unfamiliar with the task all legitimately slow results without indicating any disorder.
